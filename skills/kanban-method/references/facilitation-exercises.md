# Facilitation Exercises

Use these when the request is to actually *teach* or onboard a team to the
Kanban Method experientially, not just get planning advice. Sourced from the
*Starting Kanban* workbook's in-person exercises, adapted for direct
facilitation. Each takes 10-20 minutes including debrief.

## Flow Potential Quiz

A quick diagnostic for how ready a team's current way of working is to
benefit from flow-based practices. Have each person answer True/False,
privately, then compare:

1. Our team is highly trusted within the organization.
2. We measure the amount of delay in our workflow.
3. I'm rarely interrupted to work on something different.
4. It's uncommon for me to juggle multiple priorities.
5. All of our work is visualized where anyone can see it.
6. Our work is decomposed into small, quickly achievable units.
7. The description of what I'm doing rarely changes while I'm doing it.
8. Meetings are collaborations, not status updates.
9. It's unusual to feel overburdened at work.
10. I'm more focused on my successes than what remains undone.

**Scoring** (count of False answers): 0-1 → the team's current practices are
already close to flow-friendly, focus on refinement. 2-4 → real improvement
opportunity, expect one or two of the framework's eight sections to land
hard. 5+ → significant need; don't try to fix everything at once — pick the
single lowest-scoring dimension (usually visualization or WIP) and start
there.

Debrief questions: Are you happy with your score? What surprised you? Which
single "False" answer, if it became "True," would change the most?

## Context-Switching Game

Demonstrates the real cost of multitasking rather than asserting it.

**Setup:** Give each person a grid to fill in an odd/incomplete pattern
(e.g., a repeating sequence like Roman numeral → letter → digit → word,
"I, A, 1, Woodchuck," repeated down the page).

- **Round 1:** Complete the pattern *one column at a time* — i.e., fill in
  every Roman numeral first, then every letter, then every digit, then every
  word. This simulates finishing one type of work before switching types
  (low context-switching).
- **Round 2:** Complete the same pattern *one row at a time* — i.e., for
  each row, produce the numeral, then the letter, then the digit, then the
  word, before moving to the next row. This simulates constant task-type
  switching within a single unit of work (high context-switching).

Time both rounds and count how many full rows each person completed.

**Debrief:**
- Which round was more stressful? Why?
- In which round did you make more mistakes?
- What was hardest about the row-by-row round?
- Does this reflect how you actually work day to day?

The point isn't the raw completion count — it's naming the felt difference
between finishing one thing at a time and constantly reloading context, so
the team has a shared, first-hand reference for what a WIP limit is
actually protecting.

## Paper Airplane Game (pull system + WIP limits)

Simulates an assembly-line process with a bottleneck, then fixes it with
pull and WIP limits across four rounds.

**Setup:** Assign each person a single fixed step in folding a paper
airplane (fold in half → fold left corner → fold right corner → draw a
star → fold wing → final streamline fold → place in "Done" pile). One
person's step should be intentionally slower/harder than the others (e.g.,
drawing a star) — this is the deliberate bottleneck.

- **Round 1 — Push, no WIP limit:** Everyone works as fast as possible on
  their own step and pushes finished work to the next person regardless of
  whether that person is ready. Track: planes completed, and how much
  half-finished work piles up in front of the bottleneck step.
- **Round 2 — Same setup, discuss as a team:** Before starting, ask "will
  we produce more in round 2?" Run it again with no structural change,
  just awareness. Track the same numbers.
- **Round 3 — Introduce a WIP limit:** Cap how many half-finished planes
  may sit in front of any one person at once (e.g., 2). People upstream of
  the bottleneck must stop working when the cap is hit rather than piling
  more work at the bottleneck.
- **Round 4 — Full pull:** Nobody starts a step until the person after them
  signals they're ready for it (pull, not push). People whose step is
  idle because of the WIP limit swarm to help the bottleneck instead of
  producing more half-finished work.

**Debrief after each round:**
- After Round 1: Did output feel controlled, or chaotic?
- After Round 2: Did discussing it change behavior, or did the same
  bottleneck reappear? (It usually reappears — awareness alone doesn't fix
  structure.)
- After Round 3: Did waste (half-finished, discarded, or re-done planes)
  decrease? Did people feel more or less busy?
- After Round 4: Was the bottleneck actually resolved (not just hidden)?
  Did communication change? Is there a relationship between the
  trust to self-organize and the improvement in flow?

Round 4 is the payoff: it demonstrates that giving people the WIP limit and
the permission to swarm — not exhorting them to "work better" — is what
resolves the bottleneck.

## Facilitator scripts: the two Coordination meetings

Concrete opening lines and guardrails for someone running these meetings
for the first time (see main skill, Coordination §8, for the underlying
purpose of each).

**Kanban Meeting (daily, 15 min, standing at the board):**

Opening line: *"What can we finish today?"* — not "what did you do
yesterday." Go through the board right-to-left (closest to Done first), not
person-by-person. If someone starts giving a personal status update instead
of talking about a card, redirect: "which card is that on the board?" Keep
a visible "parking lot" list for anything that needs a longer conversation,
and explicitly schedule those conversations for after the meeting — don't
let them happen live.

**Replenishment Meeting (per cadence, ~1 hour, product manager + key
stakeholders):**

Opening line: *"Here's what we finished and learned since we last met —
what should we pull in next?"* Fixed agenda, timeboxed per item: recap
(5 min), current state and learnings (15 min), blockers (10 min), what's
next (15 min), agree on priority and sequence (15 min). If someone tries to
relitigate work currently in progress, redirect to the daily Kanban Meeting
— that's not this meeting's job. If a stakeholder who missed the meeting
tries to override a decision afterward, that's a facilitation failure to
flag, not something to quietly accommodate — it undermines everyone who
did show up.

each person or the team as a whole fill in one row per section actually
covered:

is not an action item; "add a Blocked column and review it every Kanban
