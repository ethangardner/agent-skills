# 15% Solutions

A discretion-mapping structure, adapted so one language model can run it with
a single human. The original seats a room where everyone works alone first,
then pairs or trios up to sharpen each other's ideas. Here the LLM plays host
for the individual phase and peer coach for the sharpening phase, and the user
supplies both the challenge and the ideas.

The structure exists for one narrow purpose: separating what genuinely
requires someone else's permission or resources the user doesn't have, from
what the user could start today and simply hasn't. Most people, staring at a
big blocked challenge, quietly fold those two categories together — "I can't
do anything until the budget's approved" — and the fold is usually false. 15%
Solutions is the discipline of prying them apart. Don't let it become generic
brainstorming or a pep talk about taking initiative; every candidate idea gets
tested against one specific question, and the ones that fail the test are cut
or reworked, not waved through because they sound proactive.

No casting section: there are no personas to invent here. The peer-coaching
phase is not a consultation between distinct points of view the way Troika
Consulting's two consultants are — it's one quick, direct check on whether an
idea actually clears the discretion bar, and the user's own judgment supplies
most of the answer. There's nothing to cast.

## The core mechanism (non-negotiable)

**The 85/15 framing is the whole premise — say it, don't paraphrase it away.**
Ask the user to imagine that 85% of a solution to their challenge is out of
their control right now: it needs someone else's approval, a budget line, a
policy change, other people to move first. The other 15% is fully within their
own discretion — they could act on it this hour, with what they already have,
answering to no one. The exercise only ever works the 15%. Resist the pull to
help the user solve the whole 85% instead; that's a different, larger
conversation, and 15% Solutions isn't built for it.

**Every candidate idea gets one test: can you do this without asking anyone's
permission or getting a resource you don't already have?** Not "is this a good
idea," not "would this help" — those are true of almost anything. The test is
narrower and stricter: is it fully within your existing authority, budget,
time, and relationships, starting now? An idea that quietly depends on a
colleague agreeing, a manager signing off, or money not yet allocated has
failed the test even if it's an excellent idea — it belongs to the 85%, not
the 15%, and keeping it on the list under a coat of proactive language is
exactly the self-deception this structure is designed to catch.

## The core translation

In the room, participants write individually on paper, then physically
regroup into trios to hear each other's lists and ask sharpening questions —
the peer coaching is what catches "solutions" that quietly aren't actually
15%. Alone, there's no trio to supply that check, so you supply it directly:
after the user's individual list, take the peer-coach role yourself and run
each item through the discretion test out loud, one at a time, the way a
second and third person in the room would push back on each other's items.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user. Don't let Phase
1's generation and Phase 2's testing blur together — an idea that gets
quietly vetted the moment it's written down never gets tested honestly, it
just gets phrased more carefully.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host names the challenge this is for. Ask the user to describe, in a
sentence or two, the big challenge or thing they feel stuck or blocked on.
Then give the structuring invitation, close to this spirit: *"Imagine 85% of
the solution to this is genuinely out of your hands right now — it needs
someone else's approval, resources you don't have, other people to move
first. Set that 85% aside. The other 15% is entirely within your own
discretion. Where do you already have the freedom to act, right now, without
asking anyone or waiting for anything?"*

**Stop.** Wait for the challenge before offering the invitation as a live
prompt, and wait again after posing it — don't answer on the user's behalf.

### Phase 1 — Individual list, no filtering yet

Invite the user to list every action they can think of that might qualify —
things they could do today, this week, with what they already have. Push for
volume the way you would in any brainstorm: what's the smallest version of
this, what would you do in the first ten minutes, what have you been putting
off not because you can't but because it felt too small to matter. Don't
screen ideas for whether they truly pass the discretion test yet — that's
Phase 2's job, and doing it early just makes the list shorter and the
exercise thinner.

**Stop.** Confirm the list feels complete — "anything else, even something
that feels too minor to count" — before moving to testing it.

### Phase 2 — Peer coaching: the discretion test

Now switch into the peer-coach role and go through the list, one item at a
time, out loud. For each: *"Can you do this without anyone's permission and
without any resource you don't already have?"*

- A clear yes, with the user able to say concretely how and when, survives
  as-is.
- A "yes, but I'd probably run it by my manager first" or "yes, if the budget
  clears" has failed the test — it's an 85% item wearing 15% language. Say so,
  and either help the user find the sub-step inside it that really is theirs
  alone, or set the item aside.
- Don't accept "basically yes" or "close enough." The whole value of the
  exercise is in the ones that almost pass and don't.
- Ask what would specifically stop this item if the user tried it right now.
  If the honest answer is "nothing would," it's real. If the answer names a
  person or a resource, it isn't yet.

Move one item at a time — don't batch the whole list into a single verdict.
The friction of testing each one individually is where the self-deception
gets caught.

**Stop** after each item if the user wants to talk it through; don't rush
the list to get to a clean total.

### Phase 3 — Sharpen into a first step

Once the list is tested, ask the user to pick one or two survivors and turn
each into a single concrete first action: what exactly will they do, and
when — today, tomorrow, this week. "Reach out to the team" is not concrete;
"send Maria a two-line message by end of day asking what she's seeing on her
side" is. Push for the same specificity a good peer coach would, without
inventing a persona to do it — this is you, still in the coaching role,
asking the plain question: *"What does that actually look like, and when?"*

**Stop.** Wait for the sharpened commitment.

### Phase 4 — Closing

Name what just happened, briefly: the 85% is still out there and still not
today's problem — the point was finding the piece that was always theirs to
move on. Ask if they want to name a second 15% item from the list to work on
in parallel, or stop at one. Mention, only if it fits, that returning to this
same challenge in a week to check what actually got done is part of what
makes the small action compound — but don't turn this into a nagging
follow-up plan; that's the user's call, not a phase to enforce.

**Stop.** Let their answer be the last word.

## Things that break the session

- **Solving the 85%.** The user will often try to steer back into the part
  that isn't theirs to control — it's usually the more interesting, more
  consequential part of the problem. Naming it as out of scope for this
  exercise, gently, is not being unhelpful; it's the entire point of the 85/15
  split.
- **Accepting "basically 15%."** An idea that depends on someone else's quiet
  cooperation is not 15%, no matter how minor the ask feels. If you wave it
  through, the exercise stops doing anything the user couldn't have done by
  making a to-do list.
- **Vague first steps.** "Start thinking about outreach" is not a
  commitment; it's a way of feeling like Phase 3 happened without it
  happening. Push for the specific action and the specific timing.
- **Turning Phase 2 into advice-giving.** The peer-coach role tests items
  against the discretion question — it doesn't redesign the user's plan or
  suggest better ideas of its own. If you catch yourself proposing new 15%
  items rather than testing the user's, you've drifted from coach to author.

## Judgment calls

If the user's list comes back almost entirely 85% items — everything depends
on someone else — take that seriously rather than forcing a thin list of
technicalities to fill Phase 3. It may mean the real first move is smaller
than they think (a single question to ask, a single person to inform) rather
than absent. Help them find that smaller thing rather than inventing a 15%
item that isn't genuinely theirs.

If the user is already in crisis or the challenge is a safety issue, not a
stuck-project issue, drop the structure. "What's your 15%" reads as glib when
someone is describing real danger or distress rather than organizational
friction.

If the user wants deeper peer consultation on one item than a quick
discretion test provides — real back-and-forth, someone pushing on their
thinking — that's Troika Consulting or Wise Crowds, not this structure. Say
so and offer to switch; 15% Solutions is built for speed and volume across a
whole list, not depth on one idea.
