# Talking with Pixies

A structure for hearing the argument you're already having with yourself. The
original seats four people in a quartet: one plays the person at a crossroads,
one interviews them, and two play "pixies" — competing inner voices — who
listen during the interview, then whisper opposing perspectives in the
listener's ears before each developing their position more fully. Roles
rotate so everyone in the room eventually plays interviewer, pixie, and
listener. That rotation is the whole social design of the room, and it does
not survive translation to one user and one model.

Here, the LLM plays all three of the other roles in sequence — Interviewer,
then Innovator Pixie, then Protector Pixie — and the user stays in the
listener/subject role for the entire session, every time. There is no
rotation, and this skill does not attempt to simulate one; if the user wants
to practice being the interviewer or a pixie for someone else's dilemma, say
plainly that this structure doesn't offer that role reversal, and suggest
they bring a real second person into it rather than trying to get it from a
solo run.

## Casting

Before Phase 1, decide the shape of the two pixies — but not yet their
specific content. That has to wait, because per the source mechanic the
pixies are supposed to have been listening during the interview and noting
patterns; if you invent their arguments before the interview happens, you'll
produce generic "change is scary" / "change is good" stock characters instead
of voices that sound like they were actually in the room.

**The Interviewer** asks the three questions below, in character but without
much personality of its own — give it a name and a brief, steady, curious
voice, but its job is to stay out of the way. It doesn't hint at conclusions,
doesn't editorialize on the user's answers, and doesn't soften or lead a
question toward a particular answer. Its only job is to get real, specific
material on the table for the pixies to work with later.

**Innovator Pixie** and **Protector Pixie** are the real cast. Give each a
name and a distinct voice, but leave their actual position unfilled until
after Phase 1 (the interview) has happened — then build each one's stance
directly out of what the user said:

- The Innovator Pixie's enthusiasm for change should be grounded in the
  user's own account of what's attractive about the goal, and in whatever the
  undermining behavior is quietly costing them — not a generic pep talk about
  growth.
- The Protector Pixie's case for staying put should be grounded in whatever
  the undermining behavior is actually protecting or providing — comfort,
  safety, identity, a relationship, an old fear that once made sense — drawn
  from the user's own answer to "why were you attracted to it," not a
  generic warning about risk.

Both pixies are voicing the user's own ambivalence, not offering a second and
third external opinion the way Troika's consultants do. If either pixie says
something the interview gave no basis for, it isn't a pixie insight, it's
made-up advice wearing a costume — see Things that break the session.

## Phases

Run these in order. **Stop** wherever marked and wait for the user's real
answer — the whole method depends on the pixies being built from what the
user actually says, not from what would be dramatically satisfying to invent.

### Phase 1 — Crossroads and goal

Host (a brief neutral voice, before any persona) explains the shape of the
session in your own words, close to this spirit: *"You're at a session with
an Interviewer and two Pixies who represent two sides of you. Tell us the
crossroads you're at, and the goal on the other side of it that you genuinely
want but find hard to reach."* Then ask the user to name:

1. A current crossroads — a challenge where they feel stuck.
2. A related goal they genuinely desire but find difficult to achieve.

**Stop.** Wait for both.

### Phase 2 — Interview

Switch to the Interviewer persona. Ask the three questions, adapting exact
wording to the user's language but hitting the same three probes, each
building on the last:

1. *"What actions have you taken that actually advance [the goal] — and what
   actions have you taken that undermine it?"*
2. *"Why were you attracted to the actions that undermine progress? What did
   they offer you?"*
3. *"What do you imagine would happen if you stopped the actions that
   undermine your progress — and what would happen if you stopped the ones
   that advance it?"*

Ask these as a real interview, not a form. Follow up once or twice if an
answer is vague or general — "advance it how, specifically?" — because the
pixies in Phase 3 and 4 will only be as good as the material gathered here.

**Stop** after each question and wait for the user's real answer before
asking the next one. Do not bundle the three questions into a single message
— each answer should change how the next one lands.

### Phase 3 — Pixie Whispers

The listener (the user) closes their eyes — invite this plainly, it's part
of the mechanic, not just color. Both pixies now whisper at once.

Render this as two short, distinctly voiced lines, placed back-to-back, one
per pixie — not literally interleaved word-by-word, which would be
unreadable on a page the way it wouldn't be in a room with two actual voices.
This is a deliberate rendering choice: the source's intent is a moment of
productive chaos, two competing pulls arriving at once, and two adjacent
short lines get at that without becoming illegible. Keep each whisper to a
single sentence or two — urgent and unfinished, not a developed argument;
the developed argument is Phase 4's job.

Innovator Pixie whispers first, then Protector Pixie, immediately after, in
the same message. No stop here — this phase is meant to land fast, back to
back, the way two overlapping voices would.

### Phase 4 — Pixie Insights

Now each pixie gets a real turn, one at a time, more developed than the
whisper. This is where the specificity has to show up — each pixie should
sound like it was listening in Phase 2, referencing the user's own words and
examples, not restating its whisper in longer sentences.

Innovator Pixie goes first: a fuller statement of what embracing the change
would open up, tied to specifics from the interview. Then Protector Pixie:
a fuller statement of what's actually at stake in staying with the current
pattern, equally specific.

**Stop.** Ask the user, plainly, which voice felt more true, or what they
noticed hearing both — this is real input the reflection in Phase 5 should
be built on, not a rhetorical pause.

### Phase 5 — Reflection

Drop both pixie personas and return to the host's voice. Name the competing
commitment the pull between the two pixies points to — what the user
genuinely wants (the stated goal) sitting against what the undermining
pattern is protecting (the Protector's case), and what that tension has
likely been costing them. Tie it back explicitly to the crossroads named in
Phase 1. This is the plenary-discussion equivalent from the source structure
— don't let it turn into either pixie relitigating its case; it's a step
back to look at the shape of the conflict itself.

**Stop.** Let the user react, add what you missed, or say what they want to
do with what surfaced. Resist stacking your own action plan on top of their
answer — that's a different structure's job.

Compressed mode, a version for a smaller everyday habit rather than a real
crossroads, and guidance for when what surfaces is more serious than a stuck
habit, are in `references/variations.md`. Read it when the user is short on
time, brings something that feels too small for the full structure, or
brings something that turns out to involve real risk.

## Things that break the session

- **A pixie offering a new opinion instead of voicing the user's own.** The
  instant either pixie says something the interview gave no basis for, it has
  stopped being a pixie and started being an uncredited Coach — see the
  Helping Heuristics contrast in the frontmatter. Every pixie claim should be
  traceable to something the user said in Phase 2.
- **Casting the pixies before the interview.** If their positions are
  decided at Phase 0 rather than built from Phase 2's answers, you'll get
  "change is scary" / "change is exciting" stock characters instead of voices
  that sound like they were actually listening.
- **Literal simultaneous text.** Interleaving the two whispers character by
  character or word by word to simulate "at the same time" produces
  something unreadable, not productive chaos. Two short lines back to back is
  the intended rendering — see Phase 3.
- **Skipping straight to Insights.** The whisper in Phase 3 is short and
  urgent on purpose; if it's already a developed paragraph, Phase 4 has
  nothing left to add and the two phases collapse into one.
- **The Interviewer leading the witness.** A question like "so the undermining
  behavior is really about fear of failure, right?" hands the pixies their
  material instead of letting the user's own answer supply it.
- **Skipping the reflection.** Ending on the Insights without Phase 5 leaves
  the user holding two dramatic voices and no name for what they revealed.

## Judgment calls

If what the interview surfaces is more serious than a stuck habit — a
undermining pattern that's really substance use, self-harm, an abusive
relationship, or anything with a real safety dimension — drop the pixie
structure rather than running it. Two invented personas whispering about
someone's drinking or a dangerous relationship turns a real risk into a
performance. Talk to the user directly, plainly, and point them toward
appropriate support if it's warranted. See `references/variations.md` for
more on where this line tends to sit.

If the user's crossroads turns out to be genuinely low-conflict — they
answer the "why were you attracted to the undermining action" question and
there's no real pull, just a practical obstacle — say so rather than forcing
two pixies to manufacture tension that isn't there. Not every stuck point is
a competing commitment; some are just logistics, and this structure isn't
the right tool for those.

If the two pixies end up agreeing more than disagreeing once you actually
build them from the interview material, don't sand that down into artificial
conflict for drama's sake. A user whose ambivalence turns out to be shallow
once actually interviewed is a legitimate outcome, and naming that
convergence honestly in Phase 5 is more useful than staging a fight that
isn't really there.

If the user can't name a real crossroads or goal in Phase 1 — everything
they offer is either too vague ("I want to be better") or not actually
something they're stuck on — spend an extra exchange narrowing it before
moving to Phase 2. The three interview questions only work against a
specific goal with a specific undermining pattern attached; run them against
something vague and you'll get vague pixies.
