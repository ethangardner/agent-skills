# Business Development Outreach

A single well-written message rarely wins a relationship; a tracked series
of specific, low-friction touches over time does. This skill treats outreach
as pipeline management — draft, cadence, and tracking — not as one-off copy
writing.

## Philosophy

1. **Specificity earns attention; broadcast doesn't.** A message that
   references something concrete and true about the recipient (a thing they
   shipped, a problem they posted about, a mutual connection's actual
   context) reads as addressed to them. A message that could be sent to
   anyone in the category reads as spam, however well-written.
2. **Give before you ask, and ask small first.** Lead with something of
   value — a relevant piece of information, a genuine and specific
   observation, an introduction — before making a request. The first ask
   should usually be a conversation, not a sale.
3. **The relationship is a long game measured across touches, not one
   message.** Most outreach fails from silence after the first message, not
   from a bad opening line. A pipeline with no follow-up is not a pipeline.
4. **Cadence is decided in advance, not improvised after silence.** Decide
   before sending how many follow-ups, how far apart, and what happens if
   there's still no response — deciding in the moment tends to either give
   up too early or turn into pestering.
5. **Track the pipeline as a system, not from memory.** Who, last contact,
   what was said, next action, next date — a relationship you can't see the
   state of is one you'll either drop or double-message.

## Workflow

1. **Name the smallest real next step you're asking for.** A cold or
   lukewarm first touch should ask for something low-friction — a short
   call, a reaction to something specific, an introduction — not a deal.
   Over-asking on the first touch is the most common way to get no
   response at all.
2. **Research the recipient specifically before drafting.** What's publicly
   known about them, their work, their situation right now? (This is a
   scaled-down version of `desk-research`'s "inventory what already exists
   before creating anything new" — a few minutes of real research beats a
   template with a name swapped in.)
3. **Draft the message**: open with the specific, true observation; state
   the small ask plainly; keep it short enough to read in full on a phone.
   Cut anything that isn't earning its place.
4. **Set the follow-up cadence before sending** — e.g., one follow-up after
   5–7 business days of silence, a second after a longer gap, then stop or
   switch channel. Write this down as part of the pipeline entry, not as a
   mental note.
5. **Log every touch**: date, channel, what was sent, response (or lack of
   one), next action and date. A lightweight spreadsheet or tracker is
   enough — the discipline is logging every time, not the tool.
6. **When a touch turns into a real, concrete opportunity**, that's a
   handoff point, not a continuation of this skill: route it to
   `opportunity-fit-filtering` to decide whether to actually take it. If the
   outreach was part of pursuing a whole new market or line of business
   rather than one relationship, that pursuit should already have been
   scoped by `market-opportunity-evaluation` before this workflow started.

## When NOT to over-apply this

An existing relationship you're already in regular contact with doesn't
need a structured cadence and pipeline entry — this is for building a new
relationship or deliberately reviving a cold one, not for messaging someone
you already talk to weekly. And a single low-stakes, one-off ask (RSVP-style
logistics, a quick favor from someone who already knows you well) doesn't
need research or a follow-up plan — reserve the full workflow for touches
meant to start or grow a real pipeline relationship.
