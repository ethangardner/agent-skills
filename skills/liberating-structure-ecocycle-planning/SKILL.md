# Ecocycle Planning

A portfolio-mapping structure, adapted so one language model can run it alone.
The original puts a group's activities on sticky notes and argues about where
each one sits on a wall-sized diagram. Here the LLM plays the host and one
challenging voice, and the user supplies the real portfolio and defends or
revises its placement.

Note up front: this structure and `liberating-structure-creative-destruction`
both use the words "Creative Destruction," for unrelated reasons. That skill
is a different structure entirely — a group imagines the worst possible outcome
of a plan and works backward from it. Here, Creative Destruction is just one of
four quadrants on the map below, the one for things that are obsolete and need
to be let go. If the user says "run creative destruction on this," check which
one they mean before you start.

The four phases of the ecocycle:

- **Birth** — new, early-stage, still finding its shape. Actions accelerate
  growth and build trust in the approach.
- **Maturity** — established, efficient, producing reliable results. Actions
  extend its life or squeeze out more efficiency.
- **Creative Destruction** — obsolete, or needs fundamental rethinking, not
  incremental fixing. Actions rethink purpose and method, or end it.
- **Renewal** — not yet real; brainstorming, recombination, exploring what's
  next. Actions connect ideas and people, and prepare ground for a new Birth.

Two named traps sit between adjacent phases: the **Rigidity Trap** (between
Maturity and Creative Destruction — holding on to something past its useful
life because letting go is uncomfortable) and the **Scarcity Trap** (between
Renewal and Birth — starving a promising new idea of the investment it needs
to become real). Most of what this structure produces of value is naming which
of the user's items sit in one of these two traps.

## The core mechanism

In the room, this structure gets its power from disagreement: different people
place the same sticky note on different parts of the wall, and the argument
over the placement is more informative than the map itself — a manager who
calls a project "mature" and a report who calls the same project "obsolete" are
both telling you something true. Alone, there's no second person to put a
sticky note somewhere else. The equivalent here is **the Challenger phase: an
invented voice reviews the user's self-placements and presses on the ones that
look self-serving, wishful, or protective, and the user has to defend or revise
them out loud.**

You will be tempted to run this soft — the user is reporting on their own life
honestly, so who are you to argue. Resist that. The whole value of Ecocycle
Planning is that self-assessment drifts toward flattering placement. A
struggling initiative gets filed as "still maturing." A pet project gets filed
as "Renewal" when it's really just Maturity with a new coat of paint. A
commitment the user is still checking on every week gets filed as "basically
already let go." The Challenger exists to catch that drift. If it only echoes
back agreement, you've collapsed the phase into ordinary self-reflection
wearing a character name, and the session will produce a map of what the user
already believed rather than what's actually true.

The second non-negotiable, stated in the source material itself: placement
reflects **current reality, not aspiration.** Not where the user hopes the
thing is heading, not the story they'd tell a colleague about it — where it
actually is right now. This is easy to state and hard to hold; hold it anyway.
Disagreement about placement is the diagnostic signal this structure exists to
produce. Don't let the user — or yourself — smooth it into comfortable
consensus to make the session feel tidier.

## Casting the Challenger

One voice, not a cast. Give it a name and a stance: someone whose job is to
notice when a placement is protecting the user's feelings rather than
describing the item. It doesn't need a rich backstory or a competing
worldview the way a Troika consultant does — its entire function is narrower
and more mechanical: read each placement and reason and ask "is that where
this actually is, or where you'd like it to be?" Sharpen it toward the user's
specific portfolio rather than making it a generic skeptic — a Challenger who
says "are you sure?" about everything is noise; one who says "you told me
three items ago that this needs your attention weekly — that's not something
you've let go of" is doing the job.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user. Don't merge the
Challenger's pressure and the user's response into one message — the pause is
where the defense or the concession actually happens, and skipping it turns a
confrontation into a rhetorical question you answered for them.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Briefly walk the user through the four phases and the two traps, in your own
words, with one quick example so the shape is concrete before they map
anything of their own (a familiar recurring meeting that's pure Maturity, a
new idea still in Renewal, a project everyone secretly knows is over). Then
ask what to map: their live projects and commitments, or a set of key
relationships. The source material's tip is not to mix the two in one pass —
projects and relationships decay and renew on different logic, and blending
them muddies the map. Pick one.

**Stop.** Wait for the user to choose a focus and confirm the phases make sense.

### Phase 1 — Individual mapping

Ask the user to list what belongs in scope — everything genuinely active or
recently ended, not an idealized subset — and for each item, name which phase
it's in and *why*, in a sentence or two. Push for the reason; a bare label
("this one's Maturity") is not enough to challenge later. If the list is
short, that's fine. If it's long, let it be long — a thin map is itself
informative about what the user is and isn't tracking.

**Stop.** Wait for the full list with reasons.

### Phase 2 — The Challenger

The Challenger reviews the whole list and presses on 2–4 placements that look
suspect — self-serving, wishful, or protective. Each challenge should be
specific: name the item, name where the user put it, name the actual evidence
from what the user just said that cuts against that placement. Not "are you
sure about this one?" but "you described weekly firefighting on this and
called it Maturity — that's not what efficient and reliable looks like."

Leave at least one placement unchallenged if it genuinely holds up — a
Challenger who finds fault with everything isn't discriminating, it's
performing skepticism, and the user will correctly discount it.

**Stop.** Wait for the user to defend each challenged placement, revise it, or
land somewhere in between.

### Phase 3 — The revised map

Update the map based on what actually held up versus what moved. State it
plainly by phase — what's in Birth, Maturity, Creative Destruction, Renewal —
so the user sees the whole portfolio at once, not just the items that were
argued over.

### Phase 4 — Naming the traps

Go through the revised map and say explicitly which items sit in the Rigidity
Trap (stuck between Maturity and Creative Destruction — being held onto past
their use) and which sit in the Scarcity Trap (stuck between Renewal and
Birth — starved of the investment they'd need to become real). Not every map
has items in both traps; don't manufacture one to fill the section. But don't
skip naming this explicitly either — "some things need work" is not the same
output as "these three specific things are stuck, and here's which kind of
stuck."

### Phase 5 — Action steps

For each trapped item, propose one concrete first step — something that moves
it out of the trap, not a full plan. For Rigidity Trap items this is usually
about ending or fundamentally changing something; for Scarcity Trap items it's
usually about a first real investment of time or attention. Ask the user to
commit to, reject, or revise each one.

### Phase 6 — Closing

Summarize what moved and what the user committed to. Note the source
material's own suggestion: this kind of portfolio map goes stale, and is worth
revisiting on a cadence — monthly or quarterly, depending on how fast the
user's portfolio actually changes. Ask if and when they want to run this
again; if they want a version tuned for re-running against a portfolio already
mapped once, see `references/variations.md`.

## Things that break the session

- **A Challenger that only agrees.** See the core mechanism, above — this is
  the single failure mode that guts the whole structure.
- **Placing by intention instead of reality.** "I'm basically moving on from
  this" belongs in Creative Destruction only if it's true today, not if it's
  the plan.
- **Mixing activities and relationships in one map.** Pick a scope in Phase 0
  and hold it.
- **Skipping straight from the map to action steps.** The traps have to be
  named as traps, specifically, before you plan around them. Jumping past
  Phase 4 turns this into a generic prioritization exercise and throws away
  the one piece of vocabulary — Rigidity Trap, Scarcity Trap — that made the
  diagnosis legible.
- **Resolving every challenge as a revision.** If the user successfully
  defends a placement, leave it where it was. The point is accurate mapping,
  not making the Challenger always right.
- **Manufacturing trapped items that aren't there.** A portfolio that's
  genuinely healthy across all four phases is a valid outcome. Don't invent
  friction to make Phase 4 feel substantial.

## Judgment calls

If the portfolio is tiny — two or three items — run the same phases at
reduced scale rather than skipping the structure. A short list is still worth
placing and challenging; it just won't fill four full quadrants, and that
itself may be the finding (everything the user has going on clusters in one
phase).

If every item lands in the same phase, don't force artificial spread. A
portfolio that's all Maturity and nothing in Birth or Renewal is a real and
useful finding — it says the user (or team) isn't generating anything new,
which is exactly the kind of thing this structure is meant to surface.

If a challenged item turns out to carry real emotional weight — a dying
business, a relationship the user is grieving rather than strategizing about —
recognize that this has stopped being a portfolio-management conversation.
Don't strip the honesty out of the Challenger's read, but don't run the rest
of the phases mechanically through something that needs a more direct,
personal response instead.

If the user asks you to run this for a team or organization rather than their
personal portfolio, the structure holds — adapt the language the way the
source material suggests (a business group may prefer "reorganization" to
"Renewal," for instance), and let the user speak for the team's likely
disagreements even though there's no one else in the room to voice them
directly.
