# FAST in practice

Worked scenarios where self-respect is the thing actually at stake — social
or positional pressure pushing toward a compromise the user will regret
regardless of whether the relationship survives it. For each: the
situation, a **draft that skips FAST**, what specifically breaks it, and a
**draft that applies FAST**. The last scenario shows all three letter-sets
(DEARMAN, GIVE, FAST) operating in a single message at once — the more
common real-world case than any one of them running alone.

---

## The backdated contract

**Situation:** A senior colleague asks the user to change the date on a
signed contract so it looks like it closed before quarter-end, "just this
once, everyone does it." Refusing risks an uncomfortable working
relationship with someone more senior; agreeing risks the user's job and,
regardless of consequences, is something they'll know they did.

**Draft that skips FAST:**

> "I'm really sorry, I just don't feel super comfortable doing that, I hope
> that's okay — I know it puts you in a tough spot and I feel bad about
> it..."

This is almost entirely apology with the actual refusal buried in the
middle, hedged so heavily ("don't feel _super_ comfortable") that it reads
as a maybe. It violates Apologies (there is nothing to apologize for — this
is a request to falsify a document) and Truthful-adjacent territory too:
soft-pedaling a hard no as a soft maybe is its own kind of dishonesty about
where the user actually stands.

**Draft that applies FAST:**

> "I can't change the date on that contract — I'm not willing to put my name
> on something that misrepresents when it closed. I get that this is
> inconvenient timing-wise, and I'm glad to help find another way to
> present the quarter's numbers, but altering the document itself isn't
> something I'll do."

No apology (Apologies), the refusal is stated once, plainly, and doesn't
get restated three different softened ways (Fair — fair to the colleague by
not moralizing at them, fair to the user by not over-explaining). Stick to
values shows up explicitly ("misrepresents when it closed") rather than
leaving the actual objection implicit. Note this also borrows GIVE's Easy
manner in the middle clause ("glad to help find another way") — FAST
protecting self-respect doesn't require dropping warmth toward the person
asking.

**Watch for:** This is a case where Negotiate (from DEARMAN) has a hard
limit — offering an alternative ("help find another way to present the
numbers") is appropriate; negotiating on the actual falsification is not.
Know which part of the ask is negotiable before drafting the Negotiate
clause.

## The friend group asking for a pile-on

**Situation:** A friend group is excluding someone from an upcoming trip
over a disagreement the user wasn't part of, and is pressuring the user to
also go quiet on the excluded friend "to keep things simple." The user
doesn't actually think the excluded friend did anything wrong, but going
along would be the path of least resistance with the group they see weekly.

**Draft that skips FAST:**

> "Yeah, I guess I get it, it's probably easier if we're all on the same
> page for the trip."

This isn't a refusal at all — it's a values violation dressed as agreeable
neutrality. Nothing here is a lie exactly, but "I guess I get it" when the
user doesn't actually agree is the quieter cousin of dishonesty FAST is
built to catch, and "easier" openly names capitulation as the deciding
factor over what the user actually thinks is fair.

**Draft that applies FAST:**

> "I'm not going to go quiet on her — I don't think what happened was as
> one-sided as it's being treated, and I'd rather stay friends with both of
> you than pick a side I don't actually believe in. I'm still coming on the
> trip either way, and I'm not going to make it a thing with everyone else —
> this is just where I land on it."

Stick to values is doing the load-bearing work here — the user names what
they actually believe rather than deferring to the group's read. Fair
appears twice: fair to the group (not moralizing at them, not making it
"a thing") and fair to the user (not erasing their own view to avoid
friction). Truthful — no invented excuse for why they won't participate,
just the actual reason.

**Watch for:** FAST doesn't require making the disagreement everyone else's
problem. "I'm not going to make it a thing with everyone else" is Gentle
(from GIVE) doing real work here — sticking to a value doesn't obligate
relitigating it with the whole group.

## Layered example: declining to host again (DEARMAN + GIVE + FAST together)

**Situation:** The user has hosted a large extended-family holiday
gathering alone, unpaid and mostly unhelped, for six years running,
including years they were themselves going through a hard time. This year
they don't want to host, and expect pushback — "but you always do it,"
"it wouldn't be the same anywhere else." This is the realistic shape most
hard conversations actually take: all three objectives at stake in the same
message, not one at a time.

**Rating exercise first** (see the main SKILL.md's priority tool): objective
(not hosting this year) rates a 4 — real stakes, they're burned out;
relationship (extended family, decades ahead) rates a 5 — this isn't a
one-time acquaintance; self-respect rates a 4 — six years of doing this
unacknowledged has already worn at it. Relationship and self-respect are
close enough that GIVE and FAST both need real weight, not just DEARMAN.

**Combined draft:**

> "I want to talk about the holiday before everyone starts assuming it's at
> our place again like the last six years. [Describe] I've hosted every
> year since 2020, and this year I don't have the bandwidth to do it again.
> [Express] I love that we've been able to have everyone together, and I
> don't say this easily — I've actually felt guilty just bringing it up.
> [Validate — naming the group's likely reaction before they voice it] I
> know it probably feels like 'that's just how it's done' at this point, and
> I get why moving it feels disruptive. [Assert] But I'm not hosting this
> year — I need someone else to take it, or we figure out doing it smaller
> or potluck-style somewhere else. [Reinforce] Honestly, I think it'll be
> better for everyone if it's not resting on one person indefinitely.
> [Negotiate] I'm glad to help whoever hosts get organized, or to do a
> smaller version at our place if that's easier than starting from scratch
> somewhere new — just not the full thing solo again. [FAST — no apology,
> stated once] I'm not going to feel bad about this, and I'd rather say it
> now than grit through a seventh year resentful."

Notice what's _not_ here: no over-apologizing (FAST), no burying the ask
under six sentences of guilt (Assert stays clean), and no cold Describe/
Assert with zero Validate (GIVE). The message states one clear thing while
holding all three objectives — this is what "layered, not sequential" looks
like in an actual draft rather than three separate scripts stapled
together.

**Watch for:** A message this dense only works because every clause is
doing real work — this is the point where checking a draft against the
[reviewing checklist](../SKILL.md#reviewing-a-drafted-message-checklist-mode)
matters most, since it's easy for a layered message to accidentally
duplicate or bury a letter when combining three frameworks into one script.
