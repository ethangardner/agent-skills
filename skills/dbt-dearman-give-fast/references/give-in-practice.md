# GIVE in practice

Worked scenarios where the relationship is worth more than the immediate
ask — the ones where a badly-delivered but technically-correct DEARMAN
script wins the argument and quietly costs the relationship. For each: the
situation, a **draft that skips GIVE**, what specifically breaks it, and a
**draft that applies GIVE**. Copy the reasoning, not the exact wording — the
other person's actual words matter more than the template.

---

## The 11pm venting calls

**Situation:** A friend of fifteen years calls most nights around 11pm to
vent about her marriage, often for 45+ minutes. It's exhausting and cutting
into sleep before work, but she's been one of the user's closest friends
since college and just moved through a genuinely hard year. The user wants
this to stop being nightly without ending the friendship.

**Draft that skips GIVE:**

> "Hey, I need to tell you something. I can't keep taking these late calls.
> It's affecting my sleep and my work. I need you to stop calling this late,
> or at least keep it short. I hope you understand."

This is a clean DEARMAN — Describe, Assert, and a version of Reinforce are
all present. It will still probably land badly, because there is no
Validate anywhere: the friend hears "your problems are an inconvenience to
me" even though that's not what was said. Nothing acknowledges what she's
actually going through, so the boundary reads as rejection of _her_, not of
_the hour_.

**Draft that applies GIVE:**

> "I want to say this because I care about you and this friendship, not
> because I don't want to hear about what's going on — this year has been
> genuinely brutal for you and I'm glad you trust me with it. But the 11pm
> calls are wrecking my sleep before work, and I don't want to start
> resenting a friendship I actually value. Could we do a standing call
> earlier — say 6pm a couple nights a week — instead of whenever things come
> up at night? I'm not going anywhere, I just need the timing to change."

Same Assert (the boundary is just as explicit — "the 11pm calls" have to
stop), same Reinforce (protects the friendship long-term), but it opens with
Validate ("this year has been genuinely brutal for you") and closes with an
Easy, warm reassurance ("I'm not going anywhere") instead of "I hope you
understand," which quietly puts the burden of understanding back on her.

**Watch for:** Validating so much that the Assert gets buried — this
example works because the ask ("the timing has to change") still shows up
in one unambiguous sentence, not diluted across three paragraphs of warmth.

## The manager who reversed a commitment publicly

**Situation:** In a team meeting, a manager walked back a headcount
commitment they'd made to the user privately the week before, in front of
the four people the user leads — with no warning. The user needs to raise
this directly with the manager, who they'll keep reporting to for the
foreseeable future, and who has more organizational power in this
relationship than the user does.

**Draft that skips GIVE:**

> "You told me last week we'd have the headcount. Then you reversed that in
> front of my whole team without giving me a heads-up. That put me in a bad
> position and made me look like I don't know what's going on with my own
> team."

Every word of this is true and it's a legitimate grievance — but delivered
cold, to someone who holds more power in the relationship, it reads as an
accusation with no room for the manager to explain themselves without
losing face. There's no Interested (no space for their side) and no Easy
manner — the tone is entirely adversarial, which is a real risk when the
other person can affect the user's career.

**Draft that applies GIVE:**

> "Can I get five minutes about the headcount conversation in Tuesday's
> meeting? I want to understand what changed on your end — I'm guessing
> something came up after we talked, but from where I sat it looked like a
> reversal with no warning, and it put me in an awkward spot in front of my
> team. I'd like us to find a way to loop me in before something like that
> lands publicly next time, so I'm not caught flat-footed in front of the
> people I manage."

This keeps the same Describe/Assert (name the specific event, ask for a
specific future behavior — a heads-up before public reversals) but opens
with Interested ("I want to understand what changed on your end") instead
of an accusation, and offers a face-saving read of the situation
("I'm guessing something came up") without abandoning the actual point.

**Watch for:** This is a case where FAST matters as much as GIVE — an
over-rotation toward Gentle here can tip into apologizing for having raised
it at all. The corrected draft never says sorry for bringing it up, because
nothing here warrants an apology.

## The in-law who criticizes parenting choices

**Situation:** A mother-in-law comments on the user's parenting decisions
at nearly every family gathering — feeding schedules, screen time, sleep
training — usually phrased as "just a thought" or "when my kids were young
we...". This is a decades-long relationship the user is not going to exit;
the goal is to reduce the frequency and sting of these comments without a
rupture that makes every future holiday tense.

**Draft that skips GIVE:**

> "I need you to stop commenting on how we're raising our kids. It's not
> helpful and it undermines me in front of them."

Accurate and Assert-clean, but delivered with zero Validate, this tends to
produce a defensive "I was only trying to help" spiral rather than actual
change — because it never acknowledges that the comments come from care
(however unwelcome the delivery), only that they're unwelcome.

**Draft that applies GIVE:**

> "I know you raised three kids and you care about ours a lot — I don't
> doubt that for a second. But I need to ask for something: when you have a
> thought about how we're doing something, could you bring it to me
> privately instead of in front of the kids or the whole table? It's not
> that I don't want to hear it ever — it's that in the moment it makes me
> feel second-guessed in front of them, and I want us to be able to actually
> talk it through instead of me just feeling defensive."

Validate up front ("I know you raised three kids and you care") reframes
the comments as coming from care rather than judgment, without agreeing
that the comments themselves are fine. The Assert is still explicit
(bring it privately, not at the table) — Validate softened the delivery,
it didn't replace the ask.

**Watch for:** A relationship this long-running rarely changes from one
conversation. Set the expectation that this is a repeated, gentle
correction over months, not a single decisive script — GIVE matters more,
not less, the more times a boundary has to be restated to the same person.
