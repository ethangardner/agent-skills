# 25/10 Crowdsourcing

A rapid idea-generation-and-rating structure, adapted so one language model can
run it alone with one human. The original needs a minimum of fifteen people in
open floor space. Each writes one bold idea and a first implementation step on
an index card, then the room runs five rounds: circulate, trade cards *without
reading them*, and on a signal read the card just received and rate it 1-5 in
secret. Repeat five times, so each card picks up scores from five different
readers it never sees coming from. At the end, each card-holder totals the
score on the card in their hand — max possible 25 — and the facilitator counts
down from 25 ("Who has a 25? ...a 24?...") until the top ideas surface and get
read aloud.

None of that is reproducible with one human and one model, and it's worth
saying so plainly rather than pretending otherwise. There is no floor to
circulate in, no physical card to hide a score behind, and — the part that
actually matters — no crowd. The whole mechanism depends on volume and
blindness: many independent authors, many independent raters, no rater seeing
more than a fraction of what any other rater saw. A single model cannot
manufacture true blindness; it is the same author of every rating, whichever
persona's voice it's writing in that line.

The adaptation is a substitution, not a shrunken copy: instead of trying to
fake fifteen anonymous strangers, invent a small cast of six to eight personas
distinct enough that their disagreement is real, and split the crowd's two
jobs — writing cards and rating cards — across that cast. What survives the
translation: an idea gets read by several genuinely different sensibilities,
not one, and it's the *sum* of independent reactions, not a single verdict,
that decides what rises. What's lost, and worth naming to the user if they
ask why the exercise feels smaller than the source: true blindness between
raters, and the pure law-of-large-numbers effect of fifteen-plus independent
people. What this session gives you instead is a legible version of the same
question — does this idea land with people who don't already agree with each
other — with the added benefit that you can see, after the fact, exactly
*whose* taste lifted an idea and why. The real mechanic hides that; the
adaptation surfaces it, which is a fair trade for the scale it can't match.

## Casting

Before Phase 0, invent six to eight crowd-member personas who will do two
jobs: contribute one bold idea each, and rate everyone else's. Give each a
name and a one-line stance — not a generic label like "the skeptic," a
specific taste that will predictably clash with specific other tastes on
*this* challenge.

The source material for real tension, cast so the ratings genuinely disagree
rather than cluster:

- A skeptic who distrusts bold claims and wants evidence before enthusiasm —
  scores anything that reads like a slogan low, no matter how exciting it
  sounds to everyone else.
- A hands-on executor who doesn't care how bold an idea is and cares entirely
  about whether the first step is something you could actually do Monday
  morning — a vague first step tanks the score regardless of the idea itself.
- A budget-conscious voice who instinctively costs everything out and
  penalizes ideas that quietly assume unlimited time, headcount, or goodwill.
- An end-user advocate who asks whether the person this is actually *for*
  would want it, rewards ideas that name a real beneficiary, and docks
  anything that's really for the organization's own convenience.
- A risk-averse voice who flinches at anything irreversible, legally exposed,
  or embarrassing if it goes wrong in public — and scores accordingly, even
  when the idea is otherwise strong.
- A wild-card dreamer who's bored by anything incremental, rewards genuine
  strangeness, and docks anything that sounds like it's already been tried
  somewhere.
- A systems thinker who traces second-order effects and dings ideas that
  solve one problem while visibly seeding two more downstream.

Six to eight is the range, not a target — cast the number that gives you real
spread on *this* challenge rather than padding to eight out of obligation.
Do not let two personas be near-duplicates of each other (two flavors of
"cautious," two flavors of "visionary") — a crowd of near-duplicates rates
like one person voting twice, which defeats the entire structure.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop only where marked — those are the only points that
need real input from the user.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host gives the invitation, close to this spirit: *"Bring a bold idea and a
first step toward it. You'll write your card, a crowd will write theirs, and
then the whole pile gets rated — no discussion, no debate, just numbers —
until we find out which ideas actually landed."* Ask what challenge or domain
the ideas should address.

**Stop.** Wait for the challenge.

### Phase 1 — The user's card

Ask the user for their own bold idea and a concrete first implementation
step — the two things a real card holds. Push once if the first step is
vague ("figure out logistics" isn't a first step; "call the venue Monday and
ask about their Tuesday availability" is). This is the user's card, and it
goes into the pile exactly as written, unlabeled as "the user's."

**Stop.** Wait for their idea and first step.

### Phase 2 — The crowd's cards

Each persona writes one card of their own: a bold idea plus a first
implementation step, addressed to the same challenge. Write these straight,
in the persona's voice, but without persona commentary on each other's cards
— in the real mechanic nobody sees anyone else's card yet, so at this point
no persona has opinions about any other card. Present all six to eight as a
flat, numbered list alongside the user's, no attribution of "written by," the
same way an index card in a pile doesn't announce its author.

### Phase 3 — The rating rounds

This is the mechanism. Every card — the user's and every persona's —
receives exactly five ratings, mirroring the five circulation rounds, each
1-5, for a possible maximum of 25. Never let a persona rate their own card;
in the real mechanic they can't, because it's out circulating, not in their
hand.

- **Five raters per card, no more, no fewer.** If you cast exactly six
  personas, the five who didn't write a given persona-authored card rate it,
  and five of the six rate the user's card. If you cast seven or eight,
  choose which five encounter each card, and vary the five across different
  cards — don't let the same five-persona coalition rate everything, or
  you've quietly collapsed the crowd into a single rater with extra steps.
- **Score from the persona's actual stance, every time.** The skeptic doesn't
  warm up because the idea is popular elsewhere in the pile. The budget voice
  doesn't relax because the idea sounds inspiring. Let the stance decide the
  number, not the vibe of the exercise.
- **Genuine variance is the whole point — do not write a wall of 5s.** A real
  crowd of fifteen disagrees; that disagreement, not universal enthusiasm, is
  what makes the countdown meaningful. Most cards should show real spread —
  a 5 from the persona whose stance the idea plays to directly, sitting next
  to a 1 or 2 from a persona whose stance it runs against. An idea every
  persona rates 4 or 5 should be rare and should earn it (it genuinely
  satisfies several conflicting values at once) — if it's happening on every
  card, you're not simulating a crowd, you're simulating applause.
- **No idea is exempt, including the user's.** Score the user's card with
  exactly the same rigor as every persona's. A crowd that flatters the
  human's idea has stopped being a crowd.

Show the five scores per card and the sum, for every card, before moving on.

### Phase 4 — The countdown

Reveal results the way the facilitator does it live: starting at 25 and
counting down, naming whichever cards hit each number as you pass it — "Who
has a 25?... a 24?..." — moving briskly past any number nobody hit rather
than announcing the gap. Keep going until every card has been named; with
six to eight persona cards plus the user's, that's the whole pile, not a
top-ten cut the way a room of thirty cards would need.

For the top two or three cards, name which persona(s) gave them their
highest marks and *why* — what about the idea specifically satisfied that
persona's stance. This is what makes the crowd feel like a crowd rather than
a black-box scorer: "the systems thinker gave this a 5 because the first
step doubles as a test of the riskiest assumption before anything
irreversible happens" is information the user can act on; a bare "24" is not.

### Phase 5 — Wrap-up

Close the way the source closes its discussion: note the ideas with real
momentum (high sum, broad appeal across stances) and separately call out any
wild-card idea that scored low overall but pulled a genuine 5 from the
dreamer or from a persona whose stance rarely aligns with anything — that
combination is worth surfacing even when the total doesn't rank it near the
top, because it means the idea has a real fan, not just a permissive
average. Ask the user what they're actually taking forward — their own idea,
someone else's, or a hybrid — and whether they want another round.

**Stop.** Wait for what they want to do next.

### Phase 6 — Another round

Offer one, mirroring the source's repeatability: a new challenge with the
same crowd, the same challenge with a partially reshuffled crowd, or
stopping here if one pass did the job.

the user in one session, and deeper guidance on choosing this structure over

for one of those, brings more than one challenge, or you're unsure which
structure fits what they've described.

## Things that break the session

- **A wall of 5s.** The single most likely failure. If every persona likes
  every idea, you've written a pep rally, not a crowd, and the countdown
  becomes meaningless because it can't distinguish anything.
- **Letting personas discuss.** There is no dialogue in this structure,
  unlike Troika Consulting or Wise Crowds. If a persona starts responding to
  another persona's card or reasoning out loud with them, you've drifted
  into a different Liberating Structure entirely.
- **A persona rating their own card.** Impossible in the real mechanic —
  the card is out of their hands. Keep it impossible here.
- **Fewer than five raters on a card, or an inconsistent maximum.** The
  countdown only means something if every card was scored against the same
  25-point ceiling. A card rated by three personas and summed as if it had
  five isn't comparable to the rest of the pile.
- **Scoring the user's idea generously to be nice.** The crowd doesn't know
  or care whose card is whose. If a persona's stance would clash with the
  user's idea the same way it clashes with a persona-authored one, let it
  score low. A rigged countdown isn't prioritization, it's flattery with
  extra steps.
- **Padding to eight personas with near-duplicate stances.** A second
  "cautious" persona or a second "visionary" persona doesn't add a voice, it
  double-counts one.
- **Explaining the method, or narrating what phase you're in.** A short
  phase header carries enough.

## Judgment calls

If the user's stated challenge is actually vague — "ideas for the team" —
push back once in Phase 0 before moving on. Bold ideas need something
concrete to be bold *about*; a vague challenge produces a pile of
interchangeable platitudes that no amount of rating rigor will rescue.

With only six to eight persona cards plus the user's, the countdown will be
short compared to the source's fifteen-plus. That's fine and expected — don't
inflate the cast past eight to make the reveal feel more dramatic. The
countdown format is worth keeping even at this scale because naming numbers
in descending order, rather than just printing a sorted list, is what makes
the reveal feel like something being discovered rather than a table being
displayed.

If two or more cards tie on their sum, reveal them together at that number,
the way a real room would have two people speak up on the same count. Don't
manufacture a tiebreaker the source doesn't have.

If the challenge that comes in is actually a crisis or a decision that needs
precision rather than volume — something with a safety or legal dimension —
say so and suggest a structure built for depth on one problem, like Troika
Consulting or Wise Crowds, instead of running a crowd of bold ideas against
it.

If the user wants to rate the cards themselves rather than watch the crowd
do it, let them — hand over the pile after Phase 2 and skip straight to
whatever the user wants to do with their own read, since the automated
rating exists to serve the user's judgment, not replace it when they'd
rather exercise it directly.
