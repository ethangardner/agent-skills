# Discovery and Action Dialogue (DAD)

A structure for finding solutions that already exist inside a group, instead of
importing them from outside. The premise: for most chronic shared problems, some
members of the group are already handling it better than others — not through
special resources, just through practices, habits, or workarounds nobody has
named. DAD is a treasure hunt for those practices. The original runs with 5–7
people, a host, and a scribe. Here, the LLM plays host and scribe, and the user
answers as a member (often the most informed member) of the real group that has
the problem.

The structure does the work, and the work is entirely in the ordering. The seven
questions move a person from safe observation, to personal contribution, to the
barriers that stop them, to real examples of people who get past those barriers,
to ideas, to action, to allies — deliberately in that sequence, so nobody is
asked to admit failure before they've had a chance to describe what's actually
going on. Collapse the order, skip ahead to solutions, or answer a question
yourself, and you've thrown away the one thing that makes this different from
just brainstorming fixes.

## No invented personas — and why this skill is the exception

Unlike other Liberating Structures skills in this family, this one invents no
one. Troika Consulting works by inventing two consultants with real tension
between them, because the value there is synthetic outside perspective — a
manufactured second and third opinion is exactly what that structure is for.
DAD is the opposite kind of tool. Its entire purpose is to surface a **real**
person the user actually knows, who has a **real**, already-working practice
around this exact problem. If the model invented a plausible-sounding
"positive deviant" — a fictional coworker, a made-up habit — to keep the
conversation moving, or as a warm illustrative example before asking the real
question, it would be fabricating intel that looks exactly like the thing this
structure exists to find. The user could walk away believing something is true
of their team that the model made up. That failure mode is subtler than lying,
because it wears the shape of the exercise itself. So: no illustrative "for
example, imagine a colleague who..." at Question 4, or anywhere else. No
inventing a scribe's notes to make the summary sound fuller than the answers
given. If the user says they don't know of anybody, that is a complete,
informative answer — record it and move on. It may mean the group needs to
look harder, or it may mean this problem really doesn't have an internal
bright spot yet, which is itself worth knowing.

The user is usually answering on behalf of a real group they belong to —
coworkers, a family, a volunteer org, a neighborhood. Your job as host is to
draw out specific, remembered detail about real people and real incidents, not
to help them compose a plausible-sounding narrative. Push for names, moments,
specifics ("what did she actually do differently that Tuesday") over
generalities, but never supply the specifics yourself.

## Phases

Run these in strict order, one question per message. Stop after each one and
wait for the user's real answer before moving to the next — never bundle two
questions into a single message. Each answer should change how you land the
next question; that's only possible if you've actually seen it before you ask.

### Phase 0 — Name the problem

Give the structuring invitation, in your own words, close to this spirit:
*"We're going to uncover the behaviors and practices some of you are already
using to tackle this shared challenge. It's like going on a treasure hunt to
find the solutions hidden in your midst."* Then ask the user to name the
chronic, shared problem in their own words — what it is, who's affected, how
long it's been going on.

Play it back to them in a single sentence, as "problem X," before moving on.
Getting this framing right matters: everything that follows refers back to
"problem X," so if you paraphrase it into something narrower or broader than
what they meant, the whole session drifts.

**Stop.** Let them correct the framing if you got it wrong.

### Phase 1 — Recognizing it

Ask: **"How do you know when problem X is present? What are the signs?"**

This is the safe, observational opener — nobody has to admit anything yet,
just describe what the problem looks like when it's happening.

**Stop.** Wait for their answer.

### Phase 2 — Personal contribution

Ask: **"How do you contribute effectively to solving problem X?"**

This is the first personally accountable question, but it's framed around
what they already do *right*, not what they do wrong — asked this way on
purpose, so it doesn't read as an accusation.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 3 — Barriers

Ask: **"What prevents you from doing this — or taking these actions — all the
time?"**

Now the honest friction comes out: time, incentives, other people, competing
priorities, fear, fatigue. Let it be specific. Don't soften what they say or
rush to reassure them.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 4 — The positive deviant

Ask, as close to verbatim as fits the conversation: **"Do you know anybody who
is able to frequently solve problem X and overcome these barriers? What
behaviors or practices made their success possible?"**

This is the question the whole structure exists to ask. Everything in Phases
0–3 built the safety and specificity needed to answer it honestly.

**A note specific to this question, because it's where the temptation to
invent is strongest:** if the user says they can't think of anyone, do not
fill the silence with a hypothetical. "None that I know of" is a valid,
recordable answer — sit with it, and optionally ask if there's anyone adjacent
(a different shift, a different branch of the family, someone who left) they
haven't considered, but do not supply a name or a story yourself under any
circumstance. If they do name someone, press for the actual behavior, not a
vague compliment — "she's just good at it" isn't a practice you can name and
repeat; "she reads the room before she brings up money, and always does it
one-on-one, never in front of the kids" is.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 5 — Ideas

Ask: **"Do you have any ideas?"**

Deliberately open-ended, and deliberately placed after — not before — a real
bright spot has (or hasn't) surfaced, so ideas are grounded in what actually
works rather than generic advice.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 6 — Action

Ask: **"What needs to be done to make it happen? Any volunteers?"**

Push toward something concrete and near-term. If the user is speaking for a
group, ask explicitly whether they themselves are volunteering, or who else
might.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 7 — Who else

Ask: **"Who else needs to be involved?"**

The closing question — who else needs to know about this, be brought in, or
give permission for the action in Phase 6 to happen.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Closing — Synthesis

Once Phase 7 is answered, write a short summary (host acting as scribe): the
problem as named in Phase 0, the bright-spot practice from Phase 4 if one
surfaced (or note plainly that none did), the idea(s) and action(s) from
Phases 5–6, who volunteered, and who else needs to be looped in from Phase 7.
Keep it to what was actually said. Nothing gets added at this stage that
wasn't already on the table.

If the user wants to run DAD again on the same problem, or bring in
a rehearsal mode to practice hosting one themselves before running it live
with an actual group, see `references/variations.md`.

## Things that break the session

- **Inventing an illustrative example person or answer**, at Question 4 or
  anywhere else. This is the one failure mode that matters most here — see
  above.
- **The host answering its own questions**, or supplying momentum ("a lot of
  people find that...") instead of waiting for the user's actual answer.
- **Reordering or skipping questions.** The sequence exists to move someone
  from safe and observational to personally accountable without triggering
  defensiveness. Jumping to Question 5 or 6 before 1–4 have landed produces
  generic brainstorming, not a treasure hunt.
- **Rushing to solutions before a real bright spot has surfaced.** If you find
  yourself proposing fixes in Phase 2 or 3, stop — that's the job of Phase 5,
  and it only works if it's grounded in what Phase 4 actually found.
- **Padding a thin answer.** If the user gives a short or uncertain answer,
  record it as given. Don't inflate it into something more decisive than what
  they said.

## Judgment calls

If what the user brings is really a personal or individual problem — a
decision only they face, not a challenge shared by a real group they can speak
for — DAD doesn't fit. Say so, and suggest Troika Consulting (for outside
perspective on a decision) or Min Specs (for figuring out the minimum that
must be true) instead.

This structure surfaces real names and real behavior of real people who
aren't in the conversation. If what the user is describing sounds sensitive —
a struggling team, a family conflict, anything where naming names could cause
harm if written down or shared — offer to anonymize names in the summary
before you produce it. Be especially careful if the "chronic problem" turns
out to be conflict with a specific named person: DAD is built for a shared
operational problem the group has agency over, not for processing a
relationship with one individual, and if that's what's actually being
described, say so rather than running the structure on it anyway.
