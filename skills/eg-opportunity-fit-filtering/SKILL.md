# Opportunity Fit Filtering

Decisions about job offers, client engagements, or major project asks are
often made in real time, under social pressure — an eager hiring manager, a
client already on the call — which favors the path of least resistance over
what was actually wanted. This skill separates criteria-setting (done calmly,
in advance) from the yes/no decision (checked against fixed criteria, not
re-derived from scratch under pressure).

## Philosophy

1. **Decide criteria before the opportunity, not during it.** Criteria
   invented in the moment bend toward whatever makes saying yes feel
   comfortable right now.
2. **Fit is evaluated, not assumed from prestige, pay, or timing alone.** A
   big name, a high number, or urgency are inputs to the evaluation, not the
   answer to it.
3. **A hard-no on any one dealbreaker overrides an otherwise-strong overall
   impression.** A single unresolved dealbreaker shouldn't be outweighed by
   enthusiasm about everything else — that's exactly the situation
   pre-committed criteria exist to protect against.
4. **The purpose is a repeatable decision, not a one-off judgment call.**
   Name criteria explicitly enough to reuse and check later — "the chemistry
   felt off" isn't reusable; "no access to the actual budget-holder in the
   interview loop" is.

## Workflow

1. **Establish criteria first.** If the user already has a documented
   framework (a personal filter list, prior decision notes), use that
   instead of inventing new criteria. Otherwise, work with them to name the
   specific dimensions that matter for decisions like this one — e.g.
   decision-maker access, compensation fairness relative to role and market,
   scope clarity, pace/sustainability, cultural signals, growth trajectory,
   exit terms.
2. For each criterion, define what a pass looks like and what counts as a
   hard dealbreaker versus a soft concern.
3. **Score the actual opportunity against each criterion honestly**, citing
   specific facts from the opportunity — what was actually said, offered, or
   observed — not a general impression.
4. **Separate hard-no's from soft concerns and count them.** A
   pre-committed gate (e.g. "two or more hard-no's means don't proceed")
   should generally not be overridden by enthusiasm about the rest.
5. **Apply a felt-sense meta-check.** If the person notices they feel they
   need to prove their worth, or feels pressure to convince the other side,
   treat that as a real signal — it's often more reliable than the itemized
   score.
6. **Present the evaluation plainly** — which criteria passed, which
   didn't, and a recommendation — not just a raw score with no reasoning
   attached.

For the surrounding project-planning and ethical-obligation dimensions of a
role or engagement (once its *terms* have passed this filter), see
`swebok-professional-practice` and `swebok-management`. This skill decides on
a *specific* offer already on the table — for deciding whether a whole
market or line of business is worth pursuing before any specific offer
exists, see `eg-market-opportunity-evaluation`; for the outreach that generates
these offers in the first place, see `eg-business-development-outreach`.

## When NOT to over-apply this

A minor, easily-reversible ask — a single small freelance task, a short-term
favor — doesn't need a full criteria pass; reserve this for genuinely
consequential, hard-to-reverse commitments (a new job, a multi-month
engagement, an ongoing client relationship). Criteria should also be
revisited periodically rather than treated as permanent — if the user's
stated priorities have changed, help them update the criteria instead of
mechanically applying stale ones.
