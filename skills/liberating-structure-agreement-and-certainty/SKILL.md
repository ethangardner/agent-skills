# Agreement-Certainty Matrix

A diagnostic structure, adapted so one language model can run it with a single
user instead of a room of small groups. The original has teams cluster
challenges on a wall-sized 2x2 and argue their way to placement out loud; here
the LLM plays host and the user supplies the challenges, but the arguing —
the actual interrogation of each axis — still has to happen. Nothing about
this structure is a solo activity that got easier by having a model do it.
It's a diagnostic activity that got harder to fake, because there's no group
to nod along if the host guesses.

The structure exists because most failed problem-solving isn't a failure of
effort — it's applying a Simple-problem approach (a checklist, a best
practice, "just follow the process") to a Complex or Chaotic problem, or the
mirror image: over-analyzing a Simple problem that a procedure would have
closed in a day. Einstein's line about spending fifty-five minutes of an hour
finding the right question and five minutes answering it is the spirit of
this: the placement *is* the work. Solutions come cheap once the type is
right.

## The core mechanism: interrogate, don't guess

Every quadrant placement must come from real diagnostic questions about
agreement and certainty, asked and answered, not from your read of the
challenge's vibe. "That sounds complex" is not a placement — it's a guess
wearing the structure's vocabulary. The whole value of this exercise is that
the reasoning is visible and the user had to answer real questions to get
there. If you skip the interrogation and hand back four labeled quadrants
after one paragraph of context, you've produced a taxonomy with none of the
diagnostic weight, and the mismatches you flag later will rest on nothing.

For each challenge, run both axes as actual questions, not rhetorical ones:

**Agreement** — how much shared understanding exists about the problem and
what solving it would look like:
- If everyone with a stake in this were in the room, would they agree on what
  the problem even *is*? On what "solved" looks like?
- Is the disagreement about facts (resolvable by more information) or about
  values and priorities (not resolvable by more information)?
- Has anyone with a stake ever said, out loud, that they see it differently —
  or is the agreement assumed rather than tested?

**Certainty** — how predictable the cause-and-effect is:
- If you took the same action twice, would you expect the same result?
- Do you (or does anyone) understand the *mechanism* connecting cause to
  effect here, or are you inferring a story after the fact?
- Could an expert, given enough time and data, work out the right answer
  analytically — or does nobody find out until it's actually tried?

Ask these as real questions and wait for real answers before placing
anything. Don't lead the witness — a question phrased to fish for "low
agreement" gets you low agreement whether or not it's true. And once
placed, don't relitigate or second-guess the placement out loud; the source
material is explicit that judging *where* something lands wastes the energy
that belongs later, on the mismatch. Move on.

## No casting

This structure invents no personas — it's a solo diagnostic exercise between
host and user, not a peer-consultation structure with invented voices. Skip
straight to Phase 0.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user — the axis
questions in particular don't work as a monologue you answer for them.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host gives the framing invitation, close to this spirit: *"Let's line up how
we're solving problems with how hard those problems actually are — a lot of
wasted effort comes from using the wrong kind of approach on the wrong kind
of problem."* Then ask: *What are the real challenges in front of you right
now?* Take as many as the user has — two, five, however many — as a plain
list, no analysis yet.

**Stop.** Wait for the list.

### Phase 1 — Matrix, briefly

Introduce the four types once, so the user has the vocabulary before you use
it on their own challenges:

- **Simple** — high agreement, high certainty. Following a recipe. Approach:
  established procedure, best practice.
- **Complicated** — high agreement, lower certainty. Sending a rocket to the
  moon. Approach: analyze systematically, bring in expertise, design the
  solution through study.
- **Complex** — low agreement, low certainty. Raising children. Approach:
  experiment and probe, enable emergence through safe-to-fail attempts;
  the pattern is only visible looking backward.
- **Chaotic** — the most extreme corner: low agreement *and* low certainty,
  further out than Complex, the edge of chaos. Pin the tail on the donkey.
  Cause and effect are unclear even in hindsight. Approach: act decisively
  to stabilize the situation first, then work back toward the other
  quadrants once some ground is regained.

Keep this short — a paragraph, not a lecture. It's context for what comes
next, not the payoff.

### Phase 2 — Diagnose each challenge

For every challenge on the list, in turn:

1. Ask the Agreement questions above, specific to that challenge.
   **Stop.** Wait.
2. Ask the Certainty questions above, specific to that challenge.
   **Stop.** Wait.
3. Place it on the matrix and say why, in one or two sentences that point
   back to what the user actually said — not a restatement of the quadrant
   definition. "This lands Complex: your team doesn't agree on what's
   causing the churn, and the two things you tried had different effects
   both times" is a placement. "This is Complex because there's low
   agreement and low certainty" is not — it's the definition read back.

Do this challenge by challenge, not all at once from a single burst of
context. A user with five challenges should feel five short interrogations,
not one long form.

### Phase 3 — Current approach, at least once

Pick the challenge that matters most — ask the user which one, if it isn't
obvious — and ask what approach is actually being used on it right now:
what's the plan, the process, the standing response. Get specifics, not a
category. "We have a checklist" or "we brought in a consultant" or "we're
just hoping it settles" are all fine answers; get the real one.

**Stop.** Wait. If there's time and appetite, do this for more than one
challenge — the mismatch step is more useful with more than one data point.

### Phase 4 — Flag the mismatches

This is the payoff of the whole structure. Don't rush it, and don't let it
collapse into "here's what you should do instead" before the mismatch
itself has been named and sat with.

For each challenge where you have a current approach on record, ask plainly:
does the approach match the type? Look specifically for:

- A **Simple** approach (checklist, standard procedure, "best practice")
  applied to a **Complex** or **Chaotic** challenge — the classic waste,
  where procedural rigor gets thrown at something that won't hold still
  long enough to have a procedure.
- A **Complicated** approach (more analysis, more expert study) applied to
  a **Complex** challenge — where the problem isn't that nobody's smart
  enough yet, it's that no amount of study will produce agreement or
  predictability, because the thing that needs to happen is a small
  experiment, not a bigger study.
- Treating a **Chaotic** challenge as anything other than Chaotic — trying
  to build consensus or run analysis before the situation is stabilized,
  when the actual first move has to be decisive action to regain footing.
- Over-engineering a **Simple** challenge with process built for something
  harder — the quieter waste, but a real one.

Name each mismatch one at a time, explain concretely what's mismatched and
why it's likely costing the user something (time, credibility, a repeated
failure), and let it land before moving to the next one. Where the approach
*does* fit the type, say so — a confirmed fit is useful information too, and
saying nothing when something's already working reads as if you overlooked
it.

### Phase 5 — Adjustment ideas and reflection

Once every flagged mismatch has been named, you can offer brief adjustment
directions per mismatch — a sentence or two, not a redesigned plan. The
weight of this session is diagnosis; treat solutioning as a coda, not the
main event. If a mismatch calls for genuinely prototyping a new approach
(especially a Complex one, where the fix is "run a safe-to-fail experiment"
rather than "do the obvious thing"), say so and suggest a follow-on
structure rather than trying to design the experiment here — see
`references/variations.md` for pairing this forward.

Close by asking the user what pattern they see across the mismatches — do
they lean toward over-proceduralizing everything, or under-analyzing things
that need study? That pattern, not any single fix, is usually the more
durable finding.

**Stop.** Let them answer.

## Things that break the session

- **Placing from vibes.** If you catch yourself writing a placement before
  you've asked both axes' worth of real questions, stop and go back. This is
  the one thing that can't be skipped without hollowing out the whole
  exercise.
- **Critiquing the placement.** The tip from the source material is explicit:
  don't judge where something lands. If a placement feels off once you've
  heard more, you can revisit it later with new information — you don't
  relitigate it in the moment.
- **Rushing to solutions.** The mismatch-flagging phase is the actual
  destination. If you find yourself proposing fixes before every mismatch
  has been named and sat with, you've skipped the step that makes the fixes
  worth having.
- **Defining instead of diagnosing.** If the user gets stuck arguing about
  what a challenge technically *is* rather than how agreement/certainty look
  for it, redirect toward how to address it — the source material's own
  guidance is that endless definitional debate is a worse use of time than
  moving forward with a working placement.
- **Treating all four quadrants as equally actionable in one message.**
  Simple and Complicated challenges take a sentence each. Complex and
  Chaotic ones, and especially their mismatches, deserve more room. Don't
  flatten the pacing to make the output look symmetrical.

## Judgment calls

If a "challenge" turns out to already have a known, agreed answer — it's
Simple and everyone knows it — say so quickly rather than manufacturing
uncertainty to make the exercise feel more thorough. Not every item on the
list needs to turn into a discovery.

If the user has only one challenge, run the full sequence on it anyway —
the structure doesn't need volume, and a single well-diagnosed mismatch is a
complete session.

If, mid-diagnosis, the user's answers on the Agreement axis and Certainty
axis point in genuinely different directions than where they *expected* the
challenge to land, name that explicitly — a challenge someone has been
treating as Simple that turns out to test as Complex is usually the most
valuable finding in the whole session, more valuable than any mismatch you'll
flag later, because it explains the mismatch before you've even gotten to
that phase.

If a challenge is actually Chaotic — real crisis, not just hard — say so
plainly and be direct that the first move is to stabilize, not to analyze or
build consensus. Don't soften a Chaotic placement into a Complex one because
Chaotic sounds alarming; the mismatch of treating a Chaotic situation as
merely Complex (waiting for patterns to emerge when the actual need is
decisive stabilizing action) is itself one of the most consequential
mismatches this structure can surface.
