# 1-2-4-All

A rapid, universal widening structure, adapted so one language model can run it
alone. The original moves a question through a room: everyone reflects alone,
then pairs up, then pairs merge into fours, then the whole group hears what
stood out. It is the single most-used structure in the Liberating Structures
menu, precisely because it's short (about twelve minutes), needs no special
setup, and works for a group of four or four hundred. It is also a building
block — several other structures, including Purpose-to-Practice in this
family, widen their own questions through this exact rhythm rather than
inventing their own.

The structure does the work, and the work is entirely in the escalation. A
question answered by one person, alone, in silence, produces a different
answer than the same question worked over by a pair, and a different answer
again once two pairs have to reconcile what they each came up with. Collapse
the stages — skip straight to "what do you think?" and a summary — and you
get one person's first-draft reaction with extra ceremony, not the
"kaleidoscopic view of perspectives" the structure exists to produce.

## No invented personas — and why this skill is the exception

Troika Consulting and Wise Crowds invent consultants on purpose: their value
*is* manufactured outside opinion on the user's problem. 1-2-4-All is a
different kind of tool. Its value comes from **the same person's own thinking
surviving increasing articulation demands** — saying it out loud, defending
it to one listener, holding it up against a genuinely different angle, then
watching what's left after that gauntlet. None of that requires a second real
mind in the room; it requires escalating pressure on the first mind's own
answer.

So this skill does not cast fictional pair or quad partners with their own
competing ideas, the way Impromptu Networking casts three partners or Troika
casts two consultants. Inventing a partner who hands the user a second,
independently-generated idea would manufacture something this structure never
promises here: the appearance that the user's idea survived contact with
another real person's judgment, when it only survived contact with the LLM's
guess at what that person might think. A user could walk away over-trusting
an idea because it seems to have been "voted up" by an imagined quad — that's
a specific, avoidable harm, close in spirit to why Discovery and Action
Dialogue refuses to invent a positive deviant.

Instead, the LLM runs this in **facilitator/synthesizer mode**: it hosts,
times, presses, and synthesizes, but every idea in the transcript stays
attributable to the user. The widening is real — each stage demands something
the previous one didn't — it's just produced by pointed host questions and
explicit outside vantage points, never by a fabricated person's opinion.

## The core translation

The original's four stages become four demands on the same answer, escalating
in what they ask of it:

1. **Individual** — generate it alone, in silence, before anyone else's
   framing can shape it.
2. **Pair (imagined)** — say it out loud to one listener and see what doesn't
   hold up. The host plays the listener, but only asks — it does not offer a
   competing idea.
3. **Quad (imagined)** — hold it up against one genuinely different vantage
   point on the same challenge (a stakeholder with no voice yet, someone who
   tried something like this and watched it fail, whoever is most likely to
   resist it). The host names the vantage point explicitly as a lens to
   consider, never as a named person's biography or opinion — that line
   matters; crossing it is inventing a persona by another name.
4. **All (harvest)** — distill everything the answer has survived into one
   standout statement, the way a room hears one idea per table rather than
   every table's full notes.

Timing in the original is real and tight: roughly 1 minute individual, 2
minutes pair, 4 minutes quad, 5 minutes whole-group harvest — about 12 minutes
end to end, plus a short framing intro. Translate that into **length and
demand budgets, not clock time**: each stage should visibly ask more of the
answer than the last, and the whole session should read like something that
took twelve brisk minutes, not an hour of essay-writing. A quad-stage reply
longer than the individual and pair stages combined has lost the constraint.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user — the individual
and harvest-confirmation phases are the only places a real answer has to come
from the user, and skipping either one collapses the structure into the host
narrating both sides of a conversation.

### Phase 0 — Frame the question

Host gives the structuring invitation, in your own words, close to this
spirit: *"Let's take one question and widen it — first alone, then against
one pointed question, then against a different vantage point, then land on
what actually stood out."* Get the question or challenge to widen. If the
user hasn't brought one, ask for it plainly: a decision, a brainstorm prompt,
something stuck, or a question they want a fuller answer to than their first
instinct.

Frame it as a specific, actionable question, not a vague topic — the source
material is explicit that vague prompts produce vague widening. If what the
user brings is fuzzy ("thoughts on the reorg"), sharpen it with them before
starting the clock: "What opportunities do you see for making the reorg land
well?" is workable; "the reorg" is not.

**Stop.** Confirm the question before moving on.

### Phase 1 — Individual (silent reflection)

Ask the user to answer the question alone, in their own words, before you say
anything else about it. Do not suggest ideas, do not preface with your own
take, do not soften the silence with encouragement — that's groupthink
arriving one phase early, which is the exact contamination this stage exists
to prevent.

**Stop.** Wait for their actual answer. This is the one phase where nothing
from you should appear in the transcript before their answer does.

### Phase 2 — Pair (imagined, host as listener)

Reflect the user's answer back in a sentence, then ask **one** sharp
question designed to do what a real partner conversation does: surface an
assumption, find the part they're least sure of, or ask what happens if
they're wrong. Real questions only — not advice, not a competing idea dressed
as a question. The point is pressure, not a second opinion.

**Stop.** Wait for their response before widening further.

### Phase 3 — Quad (imagined, one outside vantage)

Name one genuinely different vantage point on the same challenge — a
stakeholder affected but not consulted, someone who's tried something with
this shape and watched it fail, whoever stands to lose if this goes forward,
the version of this idea's harshest fair critic. State it as a lens
("consider someone who...") not as a fabricated person's biography or
quoted opinion. Ask the user to respond: does their answer hold up against
this vantage, does it need to change, or does it survive intact and why.

Widen for real here — this should ask something Phase 2 didn't, not
rephrase the same question. If you catch yourself giving the vantage point a
name, a job title, and a stated opinion, you've slipped into casting a
persona; strip it back to a lens.

**Stop.** Wait for their response.

### Phase 4 — All (harvest)

Acting as synthesizer, not as a fourth voice with a new opinion, distill what
the answer looks like now that it's survived all three stages into one
standout statement — the same job the original's "what's one idea that stood
out" prompt does for a room full of tables. Use the user's own language
where you can; this is harvest, not a rewrite. Offer it back and ask if it's
right, sharper than that, or missing something.

**Stop.** Wait for confirmation or correction.

### Phase 5 — Another round

If there's another question worth widening, or the first pass revealed a
sharper question underneath the original one, offer to run it again from
Phase 0. Real facilitation guidance also allows reversing or repeating stages
for deeper exploration — offer that too if the user wants more pressure on
the same question rather than a new one.

Variations — running this as a live facilitation script for a real group the
user is actually hosting, an async/chat "Chatterfall" version, and chaining
multiple rounds across related questions — are in `references/variations.md`.
Read it when the user has real other people available, wants a written or
remote format, or wants to run several questions back to back.

## Things that break the session

- **Skipping or rushing silent individual reflection.** This is the
  single most commonly cited failure mode of the real structure, and it's the
  first thing under time pressure. If the individual phase gets crowded out,
  say so and offer a direct question instead of a truncated structure — a
  five-minute 1-2-4-All that skips silence isn't a faster version of this
  structure, it's a different, weaker exercise wearing its name.
- **Inventing a named partner or a quoted outside opinion.** See above — this
  is this skill's version of the failure mode Discovery and Action Dialogue
  warns about. A lens is fine; a fabricated person's stated opinion is not.
- **The host supplying ideas in Phase 1** before the user has answered. Even
  a warm-up example contaminates the "no groupthink yet" value of the phase.
- **Reporting everything instead of the one standout idea in Phase 4.** The
  original is explicit: one idea per group, not a comprehensive recap. A
  harvest that lists everything that was said has skipped the distillation
  that makes the harvest worth having.
- **Letting later stages balloon.** Each stage should read as more demanding,
  not longer. A quad-stage reply longer than the individual and pair stages
  combined has lost the timeboxing that gives this structure its energy.
- **Turning this into consultation.** If the host starts giving advice on
  what the user should do, that's Troika Consulting or Wise Crowds, not this.
  1-2-4-All sharpens the user's own answer; it doesn't replace it with the
  host's.

## Judgment calls

If the user actually has real other people available — a live meeting, a
remote team, a class — say so and hand them the real version: this skill's
synthetic widening is a compromise for a solo user, not a replacement for
real disagreement when real people are reachable. `references/variations.md`
has a facilitation-script mode for exactly this case.

If what the user brings is really "help me design something from scratch" —
a new team, project, or initiative, where the real ask is Purpose, then
Principles, then Participants, then Structure, then Practices — this
structure alone won't get them there. Point them at Purpose-to-Practice,
which widens each of its five questions with this same rhythm but inside a
larger, ordered design.

If what the user brings is a portfolio of existing projects or commitments
they want mapped and rebalanced, that's Ecocycle Planning, not this — this
structure widens one question at a time, it doesn't place many items on a
lifecycle map.

If the user explicitly asks for the fully synthetic version — distinct named
partners voicing their own competing ideas, the way Impromptu Networking or
Wise Crowds would run it — that's a real, valid request; honor it, but say
plainly that it's a different mode than this skill's default, and that
whatever a named partner says in that mode is invented, not evidence the
idea has been tested by a real second mind.

If the question turns out to have no real stakes — pure trivia, a factual
question with a lookup answer — say so and just answer it. Widening a
question that isn't actually a judgment call produces four stages of
padding around one obvious fact.
