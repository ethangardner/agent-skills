# Wise Crowds

A collective-consultation structure, adapted so one language model can run it
alone. The original needs at least ten people: a client, three or four
consultants in a central circle, and satellite groups of onlookers ringed
around them, all in the same room. Here the LLM plays the host, all the
consultants, and the satellite observers, and the user is the only real
participant — the client.

Like Troika Consulting, the structure does the work, and the same mechanism
sits at its center: the client is talked about, not talked to, and has to sit
with that. But Wise Crowds is not just Troika with a bigger panel. It has a
second layer that Troika doesn't: after the consultants finish, a separate set
of observers reports on the *conversation itself* — its blind spots, its
coalitions, what it avoided — rather than adding more opinions about the
problem. Collapse that second layer into "more advice" and you've built a
slower Troika, not a Wise Crowds session.

## The core translation

Two mechanisms are doing work here, and they're not the same mechanism twice.

**Mechanism one, shared with Troika:** in the room, the client turns their
back on the consultants. Online, camera and mic go off. Here, the equivalent
is that the central consultation and the satellite huddle are both spoken in
the third person, to each other, never to the user, and the user does not
reply during either one. Hold this for the entire session, in both phases
where it applies. Drift toward "you might consider" kills it exactly the way
it kills Troika, and it's a bigger risk here because there's more surface
area — four consultants and two observers is six voices that can slip into
direct address if you're not watching for it.

**Mechanism two, unique to this structure:** the satellite observers are not
a second panel giving a second opinion on the challenge. Their job is to
notice the *shape* of the consultation that just happened — who talked over
whom, what premise nobody questioned, where the consultants' confidence
outran what they actually knew, what the client's body language (or, here,
what the client didn't say) suggested got avoided. This is a different kind
of attention than problem-solving attention, and it produces a different kind
of payoff: not "here's what to do" but "here's what happened while people
were deciding what to tell you." A client who's used to being advised has
rarely had someone watch the advising itself and report back. That's the
thing this structure adds that Troika structurally cannot, no matter how many
consultants you bolt onto a Troika round — Troika has no second layer at all,
so tacking on a third or fourth consultant just makes the first layer
crowded.

If you only run the central consultation and skip or shortchange the
satellite phase, tell the user you've effectively run a Troika round with an
extra consultant. That's a fine thing to offer, but it isn't Wise Crowds, and
saying so protects the user from thinking they got the meta-layer when they
didn't.

## Casting

You're casting two different kinds of voices with two different jobs. Don't
let them blur.

**The central consultants (three or four).** Same instinct as Troika —
genuine tension, not polite variations on a theme — but aim past pairwise
disagreement toward something a two-person panel can't produce: coalitions.
With three or four consultants, some will find themselves agreeing with each
other and not with the third, a majority reading can emerge against a
holdout, or an alliance from the first half of the conversation can dissolve
once someone raises a new angle. Don't pre-assign who will side with whom —
work it out as you write the dialogue, the way it would actually happen in a
room. If you find yourself able to predict at casting time exactly how the
four will split, the split is too clean; build in at least one consultant
whose allegiance could plausibly go either way depending on what's said.

Draw the four from genuinely different vantage points, not just different
opinions on this one issue — a domain expert, someone who thinks in
incentives and systems, someone who thinks in relationships and motives,
someone who's lived through the specific failure mode the user is describing.
Diversity of standpoint is what the Liberating Structures source actually
asks for ("invite diverse perspectives, not just experts") and it's what
makes coalitions form on substance instead of by construction.

**The satellite observers (one or two).** Cast these from a completely
different register than the consultants. A consultant is chosen for expertise
on the problem's *content*. A satellite observer is chosen for attentiveness
to *process* — the kind of person who, in any meeting, is quietly tracking
who's talking, what's being danced around, and whether the room actually
agrees or has just stopped arguing out loud. Think group therapist, seasoned
facilitator, an anthropologist sitting in the corner, a mediator. Give this
persona a name and a one-line stance, same as the consultants, but the stance
should describe what they *watch for*, not what they *know*: "notices when
consensus arrives too fast to be real," "tracks whose framing of the problem
won out and why," "watches for the question nobody asked."

If the observer starts diagnosing the problem instead of the conversation,
you've cast a fifth consultant by accident. Rewrite.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user; nowhere else.
Phases 3 through 5 run one after another with no stop between them, because
none of them require anything from the user — the satellite groups are
invented too, and their huddle and report-back are still the LLM narrating,
not the user participating. Bringing the user back in belongs to Phase 6, and
only there.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host gives the invitation, close to this spirit: *"Bring a real challenge —
ideally one without an easy answer. You'll hear it worked over by several
different minds, then watched by a couple more who are paying attention to
something else entirely: how the conversation about it goes."* Then ask:

1. What is your challenge?
2. What have you already tried or ruled out, and what kind of help are you
   hoping for?

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 1 — The challenge, played back

Host restates the challenge in under 60 words and names what the user is
asking for. No advice, no framing commentary.

**Stop.** Let them correct you before the consultants ever hear a version of
it that's wrong.

### Phase 2 — Clarifying questions

Each central consultant asks one or two questions, in character. Real
questions, not advice wearing a question mark. No judgment, no reassurance —
save it, on both counts, for later phases.

**Stop.** Wait for answers.

### Phase 3 — Central consultation (overheard)

The three or four consultants talk to each other about the user and the
challenge. Write it as dialogue, 600–900 words — longer than Troika's budget,
because there are more voices, not because you get to ramble.

- Third person throughout. Not once do they address the user.
- Let coalitions actually form and shift in the writing, not be announced.
  Someone can start aligned with one framing and get talked out of it by
  something a different consultant says.
- At least one consultant should question a premise the user took for
  granted, or flag something conspicuously absent from what the user said.
- Land on 2–3 concrete near-term moves, and what would make each one fail.
  This is the same payoff Troika delivers — don't shortchange it because the
  satellite phase is coming. A Wise Crowds session that skips concrete moves
  in favor of the meta-layer has thrown out the thing that makes the
  meta-layer worth having an opinion about.

End the phase there. Ask nothing.

### Phase 4 — Satellite huddle (overheard, different register)

Cut away to the satellite observer(s), huddling among themselves — also
third person, also not addressing the user, but talking about the
*consultation you just wrote*, not the challenge.

- No new advice on the problem. If a satellite observer starts proposing a
  solution, that's Phase 3 leaking into Phase 4; cut it.
- Aim at: what pattern showed up in how the consultants talked (did they
  converge too fast, did one dominate, did two gang up on the third), what
  got conspicuously skipped or rushed past, what the *client's* silence
  seemed to provoke or fail to provoke in the room, and whether the concrete
  moves the consultants landed on actually answer what the client asked for
  in Phase 0 — sometimes they don't, and that's exactly the kind of thing
  this layer exists to catch.
- 150–300 words. This phase is a mirror, not a second consultation — keep it
  tight.

### Phase 5 — Satellites report

The group reconvenes and the satellite observer(s) share their observations
to everyone, still not to the user specifically — this is the "all-together
sharing" moment from the original, where the room hears what the watchers
noticed. Keep it to the two or three sharpest observations rather than a full
recap of Phase 4.

### Phase 6 — Client takeaways

Host brings the user back in — the client faces the group again — and asks
what landed from the consultants, what landed from the satellites, and what
they'll actually do. Consultants and satellites stay quiet unless addressed
directly.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 7 — Another round

Offer another round, up to two or three clients total, which is the natural
shape in the original (host recommends up to three client rounds). Options
mirror Troika's: a new challenge with the same panel, the same challenge with
a reshuffled panel, or stopping here if one round did the job.

Further variations — round-robin staging for multiple clients, the fully
synthetic mode where the LLM also plays the client, a compressed version, and
guidance on when to reach for this structure instead of Troika — are in
`references/variations.md`. Read it when the user asks for one of those, when
they've got more than one challenge to bring, or when you're not sure whether
Troika or Wise Crowds is the better fit for what they've described.

## Things that break the session

- **Turning the satellites into a fifth and sixth consultant.** This is the
  most likely failure mode, precisely because it's the path of least
  resistance — you already wrote consultant dialogue, and it's easy to keep
  writing more of the same thing with different names. The satellite phase
  has to sound like a different kind of attention, or it isn't earning its
  place in the structure.
- **Advice before Phase 3.** Same as Troika. Clarifying questions that smuggle
  in solutions turn Phase 2 into theater.
- **Consultants who agree, or coalitions decided in advance.** Four voices
  that all land in the same place have wasted two of themselves.
- **A satellite report that just repeats the consultants' conclusions.** If
  the meta-layer only echoes the content layer, you've written Phase 3 twice.
- **Explaining the method, or narrating what phase you're in and why.** A
  short header is enough.
- **Asking permission between phases.** The two Stops are the only pauses
  that exist. Everything else runs straight through.
- **Softening the satellite observations.** An observer who won't name what
  the group actually avoided is worth nothing — that's the entire reason this
  layer exists.

## Judgment calls

If the challenge is a crisis rather than a problem with room for judgment,
drop the structure and talk to the user directly, the same call as in Troika.

If the user only wants a quick gut-check, don't reach for four consultants
and a satellite layer — point them at Troika instead, or use the compressed
version in the variations file. Wise Crowds earns its extra length on
challenges substantial enough that a second layer of observation has
something real to find; on a small question, the satellite phase will have
nothing to say and will pad rather than add.

If, partway through casting, three consultants turn out to say everything a
fourth would have said, run with three. The source treats three to four as
the range for a reason — there's no requirement to hit four just because it's
available.

If the user starts replying during Phase 3 or Phase 4, don't scold them.
Absorb what they said and have the relevant group continue talking about it
in the third person, the same recovery as Troika.
