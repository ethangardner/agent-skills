# Purpose-to-Practice

A launch-design structure, adapted so one language model can run it with a
single user. The original seats a room full of stakeholders around five
questions and widens each one through 1-2-4-All — individual reflection,
pairs, quartets, plenary — before the group commits it to a shared chart. Here
the LLM plays host and the user supplies the content directly; there is no
persona to invent and no group to widen through, so the widening is replaced
by something else: **pressure**. You press on each answer instead of letting
a room's cross-talk do it for you.

The structure does the work. It is not a project-planning template with nicer
headings — if you let the user answer all five questions in one message and
move straight to "here's your plan," you have collapsed the thing that makes
P2P worth running at all. What makes it work is two mechanisms, and both are
non-negotiable.

## The core mechanism

**First: strict order.** Purpose, then Principles, then Participants, then
Structure, then Practices. Never any other order, and never skip ahead. This
is not a stylistic preference — it is the single most commonly cited failure
mode of this structure in practice. Every group wants to jump straight to
Practices, because "what will we actually do" feels like the real work and
everything before it feels like throat-clearing. It isn't. An initiative that
starts from Practices is an initiative that backed into a purpose to justify
activities someone already wanted to do. Hold the line here even when the
user visibly wants to skip to the fun part.

**Second: every later element gets checked against Purpose before it's
finalized.** Not as a courtesy step — as the actual design discipline. Once
Purpose exists, Principles, Participants, Structure, and Practices each have
to answer to it: does this genuinely serve the Purpose we just wrote, or is
it something generic that would sit just as comfortably on a different
initiative's chart? A Principle that could belong to any initiative is not a
principle, it's a value statement, and value statements don't change what
anyone does on a Tuesday. Do this check explicitly and out loud, every time,
not silently in your own reasoning.

The two mechanisms compound: because Purpose comes first and stays load-bearing,
sharpening it is the highest-leverage thing you do in the whole session. Spend
disproportionate time there. A weak Purpose that slips through creates weak
everything downstream, because there's nothing sharp left to check against.

Revisiting is allowed and expected — the source structure explicitly makes
room for later elements to surface tensions that send you back to revise an
earlier one. That's different from skipping ahead. Going back to sharpen
Purpose after Structure reveals a contradiction is the structure working.
Drafting Practices before Purpose exists is the structure failing.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user. Do not merge
phases — a message that both asks the question and drafts the answer has
done the user's thinking for them, which defeats a structure whose entire
value is forcing them to do it.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host gives the structuring invitation, in your own words, close to this
spirit: *"Before we get to what you'll do, let's design why it matters, what
governs it, who's in, how it's organized, and only then what you'll actually
do — in that order, on purpose."* Then ask:

1. What's being started? (a team, a project, a community, a program, a
   company — get the shape of the thing)
2. Where is it right now — a blank page, or something already partly running
   that you want to design properly for the first time?

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 1 — Purpose

Ask: *Why does this work matter to us, and to the larger community around
it?*

This is the phase that gets the most back-and-forth, on purpose. Do not
accept the first draft. First drafts of Purpose are almost always either a
restatement of the activity ("our purpose is to build X") or a platitude
("our purpose is to make things better for people"). Neither is a purpose.
Push for specificity with questions like: *who is worse off if this doesn't
exist, what becomes possible that wasn't, why does this need to exist now
rather than never or later, and why does it need to be this group doing it
rather than someone else?*

Keep pressing until the Purpose is something that could only belong to this
initiative — sharp enough that you could hand it to a stranger and they'd be
able to tell a good Practice from a bad one just from reading it.

**Stop.** Do not move on until Purpose is genuinely sharp, not generic. If
after real effort it still won't sharpen, see Judgment calls below before
forcing it through.

### Phase 2 — Principles

Ask: *What rules or values must we follow to progress toward our purpose?
What boundaries do we want to set?*

Get a first list, then run the check: for each proposed principle, ask what
someone would concretely do differently because of it. If the answer is
"nothing in particular, it's just a good value to hold," it isn't a principle
for this initiative — cut it or sharpen it until it has teeth. Then check the
survivors explicitly against Purpose: does this rule exist because it serves
the Purpose you just wrote, or would it sit just as comfortably bolted onto
an unrelated initiative? Keep only what earns its place.

**Stop.** Wait for the user's read before finalizing.

### Phase 3 — Participants

Ask: *Who must be included to achieve our purpose? Who needs to be at the
table, not just kept informed?*

Push past the obvious and convenient answer — the people already in the room,
the usual stakeholders, whoever's easiest to loop in. The source structure's
explicit tip here is to expand thinking beyond the default list. Ask who is
affected by this initiative but has no voice in it yet, and who has knowledge
or standing this can't succeed without.

Check against Purpose: is this person or group here because the Purpose
genuinely requires them, or because it would be awkward or political to leave
them out? Necessity, not convenience, is the bar.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 4 — Structure

Ask: *How should we organize ourselves? How is control and decision-making
distributed?*

Resist the pull toward structural minutiae — org charts, titles, reporting
lines. That's not what this question is asking. The source tip is to
explore decentralized patterns that put control near the people doing the
work, rather than defaulting to a top-down design because it's the familiar
shape. Ask what decisions need to be made close to the work versus centrally,
and why.

Check against Purpose: does this structure make the Purpose easier to pursue,
or is it just the default org shape transplanted in because nobody questioned
it?

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 5 — Practices

Ask: *What will we actually do or offer, and how?*

Only now. If the user tried to answer this back in Phase 0 or 1, this is
where that material finally belongs — bring it back in and test it here,
rather than treating it as already settled.

Check twice: against Purpose (does this activity actually advance it, or is
it something the initiative would do regardless of purpose?) and against
Principles (does this practice violate a rule the group just committed to, or
does it live comfortably inside the boundaries set?). A practice that fails
either check gets cut or reworked, not grandfathered in because someone's
attached to it.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 6 — Closing

Bring all five elements together into one clean, short statement of the
whole chart — Purpose, Principles, Participants, Structure, Practices, each
in a sentence or two. Then ask the reflection questions the source structure
suggests: *what stands out, so what does that mean, now what will you do?*

Close with a concrete note on when the group will revisit this chart. P2P
produces a first draft, not a permanent artifact — initiatives drift, and the
source structure explicitly builds in a follow-up planning step for this
reason. Get a real date or trigger ("revisit after the first month," "revisit
when we hit 50 members"), not "we'll check in sometime."

Optional pressure-test personas and a retrofit/audit mode for auditing an
initiative that never went through this process are in
`references/variations.md`. Read it when the user wants their draft
challenged, or when they're describing something already running that they
suspect never had a real Purpose.

## Things that break the session

- **Racing to Practices.** The single most common failure. If the user keeps
  steering toward "okay but what do we actually do," acknowledge the pull and
  hold the line anyway — that phase is coming, in order.
- **Principles with no teeth.** A principle you can't translate into a
  behavioral difference is a value statement wearing a principle's clothes.
  Cut it rather than let it pad the list.
- **Participants chosen by convenience or politics.** "They're already
  involved" and "it would be awkward to exclude them" are not the same as
  "the purpose requires them." Only the latter belongs on the list.
- **Skipping the check-against-Purpose step.** Every element after Purpose
  gets checked explicitly, out loud. Silently assuming it's fine is the same
  as not checking.
- **Accepting the first draft of Purpose.** It is almost never sharp on the
  first pass. Treat a fast, easy Purpose with suspicion, not relief.

## Judgment calls

If Purpose genuinely will not sharpen after real, repeated effort, say so
plainly: the initiative may not be ready to be designed yet. Don't force a
weak Purpose through just to keep the session moving — everything downstream
depends on it, and a weak foundation produces a chart that looks complete but
isn't load-bearing. It's fine to end the session with "come back to this once
you can answer why this matters" rather than a full five-element chart built
on sand.

If the user arrives with a Purpose that's already mature and well-tested —
something they've clearly lived with and refined, not a first draft — don't
put it through the same adversarial pressure as a fresh one. Validate it,
sharpen the wording if there's real slack in it, and move on. The goal is a
sharp Purpose, not a demonstration of scrutiny for its own sake.
