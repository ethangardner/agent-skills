# Market Opportunity Evaluation

Pursuing a new market or line of business costs real time and relationship
capital before any revenue arrives, and that cost is easy to underestimate
because it's paid gradually while the payoff is still hypothetical. This
skill evaluates the *category* — is this worth pursuing at all — upstream of
any specific deal, using real evidence rather than enthusiasm.

## Philosophy

1. **Evaluate the category, not a deal.** "Should I pursue government
   agencies migrating to token-based design systems" is a market-level
   question, evaluated once, that then justifies many individual outreach
   touches. Once a specific offer exists from that pursuit, that offer gets
   its own decision — see `eg-opportunity-fit-filtering` — this skill doesn't
   replace that step, it comes before it.
2. **Ground the evaluation in real demand evidence, not enthusiasm.**
   Comparable deals that have actually closed (yours or others'), budget
   lines that actually exist, people who have actually asked for this — not
   "this seems like it should be in demand." Apply `eg-desk-research`
   discipline here: inventory what's actually known before concluding
   anything.
3. **A right-to-win matters as much as an open door.** An accessible market
   with no differentiation for you specifically is often a worse bet than a
   harder market where you have a real, specific advantage — existing
   relationships, credibility, a capability others don't have. Access
   without an edge tends to lose to whoever already has one.
4. **Price the pursuit cost honestly.** Time spent researching, reaching
   out, and building credibility in a new category is time not spent
   elsewhere. Estimate it in real terms (hours/month, months to first
   plausible deal) rather than treating pursuit as free until it either
   works or doesn't.
5. **Set the stop condition before starting, not after sunk cost
   accumulates.** A pursuit with no pre-defined checkpoint or kill criterion
   tends to keep absorbing effort past the point it should have been
   stopped, because stopping later always feels like wasting what's already
   been spent.

## Workflow

1. **Name the category precisely.** Not "grow the business" — a specific,
   bounded market or partnership type specific enough that you could say
   whether a given prospect is in it or not.
2. **Gather real demand evidence.** Comparable deals (yours or visibly
   others'), direct signals (people asking, budget lines, RFPs in this
   category), and their absence — a category with real demand usually has
   *some* visible trace of it already; a total absence of evidence is
   itself evidence.
3. **Name the specific right-to-win**, if one exists: what advantage do you
   actually have here — relationships, track record, a capability that's
   rare — versus what's needed to compete for real. If the honest answer is
   "none yet," that changes the pursuit cost estimate (step 4), it doesn't
   necessarily kill the pursuit.
4. **Estimate pursuit cost realistically**: expected time investment,
   realistic time-to-first-plausible-deal, and what else that time would
   otherwise go toward.
5. **Set explicit go/no-go criteria and a checkpoint** before starting —
   what would you need to see by when to keep going, and what would tell
   you to stop. Write both down; a checkpoint that isn't written down
   tends not to trigger a real re-evaluation when it arrives.
6. **On a "go"**, this is the handoff into `eg-business-development-outreach`
   for the specific prospects/partners in this category. On a "no" or "not
   yet," say what would need to change for the answer to flip, so the
   decision can be revisited on new evidence rather than re-litigated from
   scratch.

## When NOT to over-apply this

A single concrete opportunity already in hand doesn't need a market-level
evaluation — that's `eg-opportunity-fit-filtering`. And an already-proven,
established line of business doesn't need this either; reserve it for
genuinely new territory where the demand and the right-to-win are both
real open questions, not settled ones.
