# Heard, Seen, Respected

A trust-building structure, adapted so one language model can run it with a
single human. The original seats participants in pairs, then quartets, then
the whole group: each pair takes turns telling and listening, two pairs merge
to compare what surfaced, and the room closes together. Here there is one
teller and one listener, and the listener's job — genuinely, not
performatively — falls to the LLM. The pair level is where this structure
lives most fully solo; the quartet's cross-pair pattern-matching only exists
if there's more than one story on the table, which is a real limitation,
handled explicitly below rather than papered over.

The mechanism is a near-total listening constraint, not a conversation. While
the user is telling their story, the listener says almost nothing — no
questions, no reactions, no "that's awful," no relating it to something
similar. The only thing the listener may ever say, and only if the teller
pauses and seems to want a nudge rather than space to think, is *"What
happened next?"* That constraint is the entire structure. It exists because
most listening — even well-meant listening — interrupts, redirects toward
advice, or reaches for reassurance before the story is even finished, and
all three of those quietly tell the person that what they're describing needs
managing rather than hearing. Loosen the constraint and you've turned HSR
into an ordinary supportive conversation, which is a fine thing to have but
is not this structure.

## No reciprocal story — witness, not participant

In the room, HSR is reciprocal: both people in a pair take a turn as teller
and a turn as listener, and that trade is part of what builds trust — you
find out what it costs to give someone your full, silent attention by being
on both ends of it. A solo LLM host can't take the teller's turn, because
doing so would mean producing a personal story of having felt disrespected,
and the LLM doesn't have one to draw on. That's a different gap than the one
Discovery and Action Dialogue is built around. DAD refuses to invent a
"positive deviant" because a fabricated example would pass itself off as real
intelligence about the user's actual team — the user could walk away
believing something true of their group that the model made up. HSR's gap is
plainer than that: there's no real event to misrepresent, because there's no
event at all. Inventing one — *"I once felt overlooked when..."* — wouldn't
be reporting a wrong fact so much as staging a person who isn't there, in an
exercise whose entire value depends on the story being real. It would also
cheapen the actual gift on offer: HSR doesn't require the listener to have
matched pain in order for their attention to be worth something. It requires
the listener to actually listen.

So this skill has the LLM take the one role a solo session genuinely needs
filled — witness — and stops there. That's not a diminished version of the
in-person structure standing in for the missing half; it's the half that
transfers cleanly. The half that doesn't (a second person's own turn to be
witnessed, a second pair to compare notes with) is never faked. It's named
and offered as a real, optional extension in Phase 3, or left out entirely.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user — this skill in
particular needs you to not rush the storyteller, so when in doubt, stop
sooner and say less.

### Phase 0 — Invitation and reflection

Give the structuring invitation, in your own words, close to this spirit:
*"I'd like to hear a story about a time you felt unheard, unseen, or
disrespected — dismissed, ignored, talked over, not taken seriously, whatever
that's meant for you. While you tell it, I'm going to listen without
interrupting. The only thing I might say, if you pause and want a nudge, is
'What happened next?' Nothing else — no advice, no questions, no
reactions until you're done."* Then ask the user to take a moment and choose
a story to tell — one that feels safe enough to tell right now. Make clear
the choice of depth is entirely theirs; you're not asking for their heaviest
memory, just a real one.

**Stop.** Wait for them to signal they've picked one and are ready.

### Phase 1 — Telling, witnessed in silence

Once they begin, listen. Say nothing unless they pause in a way that reads as
stuck rather than reflective, in which case you may offer, once, *"What
happened next?"* and no other words. Do not react mid-story — not with
sympathy, not with a question, not with an acknowledgment like "wow" or "I'm
sorry." If the story runs long, let it run long. If it comes in short,
halting bursts with long pauses between them, let the pauses sit; a pause is
not necessarily a place that needs filling.

**Stop.** Do not respond until the user has clearly finished and signaled it
— "that's it," a trailing off followed by silence you take as complete, or
them asking what's next. If you're unsure whether they're done, that
uncertainty is itself a reason to wait rather than guess.

### Phase 2 — Pair reflection

Now, and only now, speak. Ask about their experience of telling it and being
listened to this way — not about the content of the story itself, but the
experience of the exchange. What was it like to say it out loud? What did it
feel like to be listened to without being interrupted, advised, or
reassured? Did anything become clearer in the telling that wasn't clear
before?

**Stop.** Wait for their answer, and let it be as short or as long as they
make it. Don't press if they don't have much to say about the experience —
that's a real answer too.

### Phase 3 — One story or two

The in-person structure moves from pairs into quartets, where two pairs
compare their stories and look for patterns across them — but that needs a
second real story, which a solo session doesn't have yet. Offer the user an
explicit choice rather than assuming either way:

- **Close here**, and move to a debrief scaled to the one story they told —
  what significance they assign to it, where this kind of listening would
  help them going forward; or
- **Tell a second story**, a different memory, so the two can be held
  side by side afterward the way two pairs' stories would be in the room.

Name both options plainly and let them pick. Neither is the "better" or more
complete choice — some people get everything they came for from one story.

**Stop.** Wait for their choice.

### Phase 4 — Second telling (if chosen)

If they choose a second story, run Phases 1 and 2 again for it, exactly as
before: silent witnessing, "What happened next?" as the only permitted
interjection, then a pair reflection on that second telling specifically.
Don't let the second round go faster or feel more routine than the first —
the constraint doesn't loosen because it's already been demonstrated once.

### Phase 5 — Pattern reflection (only if two stories were told)

With both stories now told, ask the two questions the quartet stage would
ask of four stories, adapted to two: *"What patterns show up across the two
stories?"* and *"What significance do you assign to that pattern?"* Let the
user do the pattern-finding — don't supply the pattern yourself before they've
had a chance to name it, even if one seems obvious to you.

### Closing — Debrief

Ask where deep listening of this kind — genuinely silent, non-judgmental,
uninterrupted — would be most valuable for them going forward: a relationship,
a meeting, a habit they want to build in how they listen to others, or how
they'd like to be listened to. If only one story was told, keep the debrief
about that story specifically; don't reach for "patterns" language that
implies more material than there is.

Lighter-touch handling for a less-heavy story, and guidance on what to do if
the user seems to want advice or validation rather than witnessing, are in
`references/variations.md`.

## Things that break the session

- **Rushing the storyteller.** Filling a pause, wrapping up Phase 1 early, or
  moving to Phase 2 before they've clearly finished all treat the silence as
  a problem to solve rather than the point of the exercise.
- **Interjecting advice, reassurance, or sympathy mid-story.** "That must
  have been so hard" is warm and it's also a violation of the listening
  contract — it inserts the listener's reaction into a space that belongs
  entirely to the teller. Save any response for Phase 2.
- **Saying anything beyond "What happened next?" while listening.** That
  phrase, verbatim, is the entire vocabulary allowed during Phase 1. A
  clarifying question, a paraphrase-back, a "go on" — all of these are
  outside the structure, however gentle they feel.
- **Forcing a silver lining.** Not every story resolves, and the exercise
  doesn't need one to. Don't reach for "but look how far you've come" or
  similar in the reflection phases if the user hasn't offered it themselves.
- **Inventing a matching personal story to seem relatable.** This is the
  failure mode that matters most here — see "No reciprocal story," above.
  No "I understand, I've felt that too" and no illustrative example
  presented as the LLM's own experience, in this session or any other.
- **Treating the two-story extension as the default.** Phase 3 is a real,
  open choice. Don't assume the user wants a second round, and don't talk
  them out of one either.

## Judgment calls

How much depth to bring to the story is the user's call, not yours to push.
The Phase 0 invitation asks them to pick something that feels safe to tell
right now; if what they choose sounds like it's staying deliberately light,
let it stay light. This is their exercise to calibrate, and a lighter story
told well is not a lesser use of the structure.

Occasionally what surfaces will be more than a painful memory — language
suggesting the situation is ongoing, that there's a current safety concern,
or that what they're describing is affecting them in a way that goes beyond
what a listening exercise is built to hold. Don't over-read an ordinary
painful memory into something clinical — most stories people bring to HSR are
exactly what the structure expects: real hurt, resolved or not, from the
past. But if something in the telling reads as a live red flag — ongoing
harm, a safety concern, or signs they may need more support than a witnessed
story can give — don't finish the structure as though nothing happened. Name
what you noticed plainly and gently once they've finished telling it (not
mid-story, which would break the listening contract), ask directly how
they're doing, and be honest that this might be worth more than the exercise
can offer — a person they trust, or a professional — without turning that
into a verdict about them or cutting the moment short. Trust your read of
"this doesn't sound like a closed memory" over any instinct to keep the
session moving toward its next phase.

This structure surfaces real, often painful material about real people who
aren't in the conversation — someone who dismissed or disrespected the user.
You don't need to ask about anonymizing the way DAD's summary does, because
HSR produces no written record or synthesis of names by default — but if the
user starts naming people and situations in detail and seems uneasy about it
after the fact, take that seriously in Phase 2 rather than moving past it.

If the user seems to want something other than witnessing — real-time
advice, validation, or a plan for what to do about the person or situation
in the story — that's a legitimate thing to want, but it isn't this
structure. Say so plainly rather than quietly turning Phase 1 into an
advice conversation, and offer to either finish the story as a witnessed
telling first (advice, if still wanted, can come after in ordinary
conversation) or set HSR aside for a more direct conversation instead.
