# Wicked Questions

A paradox-naming structure, adapted so one language model can run it alone with
a single human. The original seats a group who each list truths about a shared
challenge on paper, circle two that feel opposed, and forge them into one
sentence using a fixed template — then a small table of peers stress-tests that
sentence until it lands with an actual "arrrgh." Here the LLM plays host and
the table both, and the user supplies the truths and the judgment about which
tension is real.

No casting section: there are no personas to invent here, same as Min Specs
and the Agreement-Certainty Matrix. Wicked Questions isn't a consultation
between distinct points of view — it's the user's own situation, examined
until the real fault line in it becomes visible, so there's nothing to cast.

## The core mechanism: a paradox, not a dilemma

A Wicked Question is not "should we prioritize speed or quality" wearing
fancier language. A dilemma has a right answer once you think hard enough
about tradeoffs. A wicked question doesn't — both sides are simultaneously
**true and necessary**, and the situation demands both at once, permanently,
not as a temporary phase to get through. That's what makes it wicked instead
of merely hard.

The test for whether you've found one: when you say the sentence out loud, it
should produce something closer to a groan of recognition than a nod. The
source material calls this the "arrrgh" response — a felt sense of *oh no,
we really are trying to do both of those, aren't we.* If the user's reaction
to a drafted question is calm agreement or a shrug, it isn't wicked yet. Go
back and sharpen it, or find a different pair of truths.

**Both truths have to survive scrutiny as genuinely true, not just as a
grievance and its opposite.** A pairing like "we move too fast and we're too
timid" is often really just one complaint about inconsistency, not two forces
the situation legitimately needs at the same time. Before locking in a pair,
ask: is there a real, current, defensible case for *each* side being
necessary — not just present, but actually good and worth keeping? If one
side is just a flaw to be corrected, this isn't a wicked question, it's a
problem with an answer. Don't force it into the template anyway.

## The core translation

In the room, a table of four to six people push a drafted question back and
forth, sharpening the wording and testing whether it actually lands, and the
"arrrgh" is a real, audible thing that happens or doesn't across several
people at once. Alone, you are that table. Your job in the refinement step is
to be the skeptical peer who says "that's not quite it yet" rather than
accepting the first draft — and to ask the grounding question the source
material recommends: *when have you actually witnessed both of these forces
at play at the same time, even briefly?* An answer that's all abstraction and
no lived instance is a sign the pairing hasn't been tested against reality
yet.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user — the truth list
and the tension pair in particular don't work as a monologue you fill in for
them.

### Phase 0 — Intake

Host gives the framing invitation, close to this spirit: *"Some of the
hardest situations aren't a problem with one right answer — they're two
things pulling in opposite directions that you actually need both of. Let's
find the real tension underneath what you're dealing with, and name it as one
question."* Then ask: *What's the challenge or situation you want to look at?*
Take it as a plain description — no analysis yet.

**Stop.** Wait.

### Phase 1 — List the truths

Ask the user to list truths about the challenge — as many as they can, mixed
together with no filtering or sorting yet. Truths here means real, current
statements about the situation: forces at play, things that are working,
things that are working against each other, obstacles, and opportunities
alike. Push for volume the way you would in a brainstorm — prompt for what's
pulling people toward one behavior, what's pulling them toward the opposite,
what the stated goal is versus what actually happens day to day, what would
be lost if either force disappeared entirely.

Don't sort into good/bad or for/against while the list is being built. A
truth about a real obstacle sits on the same list as a truth about a real
strength, unsorted, until this step is done.

**Stop.** Confirm the list feels complete before moving on.

### Phase 2 — Circle the tension

Look across the list with the user for two truths that feel like they pull in
opposite directions, and that both survive the "is this actually necessary,
not just present" test from above. Don't default to the first pair that looks
opposite on the surface — ask the user which pairing actually feels like the
live wire in their situation, the one that makes them wince a little to say
out loud.

If nothing on the list feels genuinely opposed, that's real information: say
so, and either go back to Phase 1 for a longer list or consider whether this
situation is better served by a different structure entirely (see Judgment
calls, below).

**Stop.** Let the user land on the pair, not just accept your first suggestion.

### Phase 3 — Draft the question

Introduce the template once it's needed, not before:

**"How is it that we are ___ while simultaneously ___?"**

Fill it with the circled pair. Push toward tight, ideally single-word or
short-phrase opposites rather than long clauses — "structured AND liberated,"
not "we follow a lot of process AND also we want people to feel free to
improvise when the process doesn't fit." Compression is what gives the
sentence its bite; a wicked question that takes two breaths to say has lost
the paradox in the padding.

Watch for blame creep while drafting. "How is it that we demand urgency and
tolerate people cutting corners" frames one side as a failure to be corrected,
not a force the situation genuinely needs — that's a complaint in a wicked
question's clothing. Reword until both halves read as legitimate, not as one
virtue and one vice.

### Phase 4 — Refine until it's actually wicked

Play the skeptical table. Read the drafted question back and test it:

- Does it produce something closer to "arrrgh" than a shrug? If it's easy to
  resolve by picking a side, it isn't wicked — go back and tighten the
  wording, or reconsider whether the underlying pair from Phase 2 was the
  real tension.
- Ask the grounding question: *when have you actually seen both of these
  happening at once, even for a moment?* A concrete instance means the
  question is real. If the user can only answer in the abstract, the pairing
  may be more theoretical than lived — push on it before finalizing.
- If more than one tension surfaced in Phase 2, it's fine to draft two or
  three candidate wicked questions here rather than forcing a single one
  early.

**Stop.** Confirm with the user which question(s) actually land before moving
on — don't declare victory on a question only you think is sharp.

### Phase 5 — Hold it, don't resolve it

This is the payoff, and the place this structure is most often quietly
sabotaged: **do not answer the wicked question.** Answering it — "well, in
practice we should lean toward X when Y, and toward Y when X" — collapses the
paradox back into an ordinary tradeoff and throws away the thing the whole
exercise built.

Instead, ask what it would actually look like, concretely, to do *both* at
once — not sequentially, not by splitting the difference, but genuinely
simultaneously. What practice, structure, or habit would let both forces be
true at the same time rather than fighting for turns? Treat this as
strategic idea-generation, not problem-solving: welcome ideas that sound a
little impossible at first, since the whole point of naming a paradox instead
of a dilemma is that the easy resolutions have already been ruled out.

Close by asking which of the ideas that came up feels most worth actually
trying, and what the first small test of it would look like.

**Stop.** Let the user answer.

## Things that break the session

- **Answering the question instead of holding it.** The single most common
  failure. If you or the user slide into "so the answer is to balance them
  by doing X in situation A and Y in situation B," the paradox has been
  quietly converted into a dilemma and resolved away. Notice it and pull
  back to Phase 5's actual question: what does doing *both at once* look
  like.
- **A dilemma wearing wicked clothing.** If one side of the pair is really
  just a flaw and the other its fix, this was never a paradox — it's a
  problem with an answer. Don't force the template onto it.
- **Padding instead of compression.** Long, hedged, softened phrasing on
  either side of the template kills the "arrrgh." Push for single words or
  short phrases, not paragraphs.
- **Blame-shaped pairings.** A wicked question with one virtuous half and
  one guilty half isn't naming a genuine tension, it's grading one side.
  Reword until both halves are things a reasonable person would defend.
- **Settling for the first draft.** The refinement step in the room happens
  across several skeptical peers for a reason — the first phrasing rarely
  survives contact with real scrutiny. Play that role rather than accepting
  the first sentence that fits the template.

## Judgment calls

If, after a genuine attempt in Phase 2, nothing on the list is really in
tension — every truth points the same direction, or the apparent opposites
collapse into one complaint once examined — say so plainly. Not every
situation is wicked; some are just hard, or just unclear, and forcing a
paradox where there isn't one produces a hollow sentence with no "arrrgh"
behind it. If the real issue is that the user doesn't know what kind of
problem they're facing, liberating-structure-agreement-and-certainty is
probably the better fit. If the real issue is that the future is what's
unsettling them, liberating-structure-critical-uncertainties is.

If the user arrives already certain which side of a tension is "right," treat
that certainty as data, not as a stop sign. Ask what would be lost if the
other side disappeared entirely — often the case for the neglected side is
still there, just unexamined, and surfacing it is exactly what turns a
one-sided complaint into a real wicked question.

If the drafted question is technically well-formed but the user's answers in
Phase 4 stay abstract and hedged, don't manufacture false specificity to move
things along. An unresolved "I'm not sure I've ever actually seen both at
once" is itself useful information — it may mean the tension is more
aspirational than lived, which is worth naming rather than papering over.

If the user wants to run this on a challenge that's really about
predictability or unknowns rather than competing values that are both true
right now, say so and point at the more apt structure rather than stretching
Wicked Questions to cover ground it wasn't built for.
