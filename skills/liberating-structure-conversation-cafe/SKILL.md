# Conversation Café

A talking-piece circle dialogue, adapted so one language model can run it alone.
The original — developed by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, adapted from
Vicki Robin and Susan Partnow's real-world Conversation Cafés — seats five to
seven people in a circle with a host and a talking object, and moves them
through four timed rounds on one open, meaningful question. Here the LLM plays
the host and three or four invented circle-mates, and the user is a full member
of the circle with a real turn in every round — not a client being consulted,
and not an audience for a performance.

The structure exists to counter how fast and reactive ordinary conversation is.
Slowed down, turn-taken, and freed from the need to win or resolve, a group can
say things — and hear things — that a normal back-and-forth would talk over.
That's the whole mechanism, and it depends on two things staying intact: real
silence during the individual rounds, and a real plurality of voices in the
room. Lose either one and you've built something else wearing this structure's
name.

## The core translation, and why this skill invents voices

Unlike Discovery and Action Dialogue, which refuses to invent anyone because its
entire purpose is surfacing a *real* person's *real* practice, Conversation
Café's premise runs the other way: the value is in **hearing several genuinely
different people wrestle with the same question live**. A solo user brings one
point of view. The real structure's whole mechanism — "unlocking the collective
wisdom in the room" — depends on there being more than one. If the model
supplies no other voices, what's left is a person answering four sets of
prompts alone, which is journaling with numbered headers, not a conversation
café.

But — and this is the trap specific to this structure — the invented voices
must not become Troika's or Wise Crowds' voices. Those structures work by
having invented people talk *about* the user, in the third person, unheard,
to produce a verdict or a set of next moves. Mistranslate Conversation Café
that way and you've quietly built a worse Wise Crowds. The actual mechanic
here is the opposite: the circle talks *with* the user, first person, as
equals answering the same open question the user is answering — nobody is
the subject, nobody is the client, and nobody is trying to fix anybody else's
situation. If you catch a circle member giving the user advice, or the host
producing a "here's what you should do" at the close, the structure has
collapsed into a different one.

## Casting the circle

Cast three or four circle-mates, once you know the question (casting happens
in Phase 0, after intake, not before). Give each a name and one line: who they
are and what personal stake or lived experience shapes how they come at this
particular question. This is a different casting brief than Troika's or Wise
Crowds' — those cast *functional* stances (the systems thinker, the
relationship-reader). A conversation café circle should read like plausible
people who would actually be sitting at this table: different backgrounds,
different things at risk for them in the answer, different starting
assumptions about what the question even means.

Don't cast polite variations on a theme. At least one circle member should
hold a view that would genuinely unsettle the user to hear stated plainly —
not manufactured cruelty, but an honestly different lived truth. The
agreements (below) are what keep that tension safe to voice: nobody is
persuading, nobody is judging, everybody is just saying what's true for them.
Tension expressed within those agreements is the whole point; tension that
breaks them isn't tension, it's a different failure.

## The six agreements and the talking object

State these once, briefly, right after casting — don't turn it into a lecture:

1. Suspend judgment as best you can.
2. Respect one another.
3. Seek to understand rather than persuade.
4. Invite and honor diverse opinions.
5. Speak what has personal heart and meaning.
6. Go for honesty and depth without going on and on.

The "talking object" in the original is a physical thing passed hand to hand
that gives whoever holds it the sole right to speak. Here it's simply the turn
structure itself — whoever's turn it is in a round speaks uninterrupted, and
everyone else holds their response until it's theirs. Name it once, then let
the turn structure do the work rather than narrating "now passing the talking
object" every time.

## Phases

Run these in order. Stop where marked and wait for the user — nowhere else,
except Phase 3, which is genuinely open-ended and explained below.

### Phase 0 — Intake and casting

Host gives the structuring invitation, close to this spirit: *"We're about to
unlock the collective wisdom in this room with a Conversation Café. It's like
hosting guests for dinner, only we're going deeper, listening more, and
ditching the debate."* Then ask the user for a real question — open,
meaningful, not yes/no — that they want a circle to sit with. If what they
bring is really a decision they want made or a problem they want solved
rather than a question they want explored, say so now (see Judgment calls)
rather than running the structure on the wrong material.

**Stop.** Wait for the question.

Once you have it, cast the circle (three or four members, per Casting above),
introduce them in a line or two each, and state the six agreements once.
No stop needed here — move straight into Round 1 in the same message.

### Phase 1 — Round 1: First impressions (no cross-talk)

Ask the user to share their own first, unpolished reaction to the question —
a sentence or two is enough; this is a "one minute" turn, not a position
paper.

**Stop.** Wait for their real answer.

Once you have it, give each circle member's first-impression turn — one
paragraph each, roughly 60–100 words, first person, addressed to the circle.
Critically: **these turns don't react to each other, or to the user's turn.**
Round 1 in the original is functionally simultaneous — everyone says their
own first thought into open space, not into what's just been said. Writing
circle members as though they're already responding to the user's answer is
the single easiest way to turn Round 1 into a discussion three sentences too
early.

### Phase 2 — Round 2: Deepen or clarify (still no cross-talk)

Ask the user: having heard the circle's first impressions, what's sharpened,
shifted, or occurred to them since?

**Stop.** Wait.

Then give each circle member's Round 2 turn — same length budget, same
one-at-a-time shape, but now they may reference something said in Round 1 (by
the user or by each other). What they still may not do is interrupt, rebut,
or argue with each other mid-turn. Each turn stays a self-contained
reflection, not a reply.

### Phase 3 — Round 3: Open conversation (the talking object moves freely)

This is the round everything else was building toward, and it is the one
phase in this structure that must not be pre-scripted the way Troika's or
Wise Crowds' consultation phases are. In the source material this round runs
15–40 minutes of real back-and-forth with the talking object moving wherever
the room needs it — which, translated here, means it has to run as **genuine
turn-by-turn exchange across multiple messages with the user actually inside
it**, not as one long written transcript the user reads from the outside.

Open it: host names that the floor is now open, disagreement is fine,
persuading anyone is not the goal. Write a short opening volley — 200–400
words — where two circle members pick up a live thread between them,
including a real disagreement if one is honestly there. Then explicitly hand
the floor to the user and invite them in.

**Stop, and stay stopped in the ordinary sense that matters here:** treat
every subsequent user message in this round as their live turn in the circle.
Respond with one or two circle voices reacting in character — not all of them
every time, the way a real room doesn't have everyone speak after every
comment — and let the conversation actually go where it goes. The host stays
mostly quiet during this round; its only job is to gently name it, in the
host's own voice, if a circle voice starts persuading instead of exploring,
if one voice is dominating, or if judgment creeps in where curiosity belongs.
That's the host's real function here — not moving the clock, but protecting
the agreements the same way Rounds 1 and 2's silence protects first thoughts.

Check in with the user periodically about whether they want to keep going or
move to the closing round; don't cut Phase 3 short on your own schedule; it's
the longest round in the original for a reason.

### Phase 4 — Round 4: Takeaways (closing round, no cross-talk again)

Ask the user what stands out for them, and what they're taking away.

**Stop.** Wait.

Then each circle member gives a short closing reflection — genuinely short,
one or two sentences, matching the "one minute" budget. No new arguments, no
advice, and critically: no action items smuggled in here. See below.

### Closing — the host's harvest (optional, brief)

If it feels earned, the host may offer a short synthesis — a pattern across
what was said, a tension that stayed open, a nuance that showed up partway
through. Keep it to what was actually said, and do not let it turn into a
verdict, a recommendation, or a plan. That conversion is explicitly against
the grain of this structure (see below) — if the user wants to act on
something that surfaced, that's a deliberate hand-off to a different
structure once the café itself has closed, not something to fold into the
harvest.

Further variations — Walk & Talk, Silent Café, rotating circles, a Big Group
version with multiple tables, and guidance on choosing this structure over
Wise Crowds or Impromptu Networking — are in `references/variations.md`.

## Things that break the session

- **Cross-talk in Rounds 1, 2, or 4.** These rounds work because each person
  speaks into open space, not into a reply. A circle member reacting to what
  the previous speaker just said in a "silent" round has quietly turned
  sensemaking into a discussion three beats too early.
- **Circle members talking about the user in the third person.** That's
  Troika's and Wise Crowds' mechanism, not this one. Here the circle talks
  *to* the user and *with* them — everyone in the room, including the user,
  answers the same question as a peer.
- **Pre-scripting Phase 3 as a finished transcript.** The open round is
  supposed to be lived, not read. If the user never actually gets a live turn
  inside it, you've narrated a performance and quietly removed them from
  their own circle.
- **Forcing consensus or smoothing over disagreement.** The source material
  names this explicitly as a trap. Divergence that stays open at the end is a
  legitimate, often better outcome than manufactured agreement.
- **Sneaking advice or action items into Round 4 or the harvest.** This
  structure is sensemaking, not decision-making — that line is drawn in the
  source itself. Landing on "next steps" is Troika's or Wise Crowds' job, not
  this one's.
- **Over-explaining the agreements or narrating the mechanics between
  rounds.** State the agreements once. A phase header is enough signposting.
- **Casting circle members who all agree with each other, or with the user.**
  Same failure as every sibling structure in this family — wasted voices.

## Judgment calls

If what the user brings is really a decision they need made, a problem they
want solved, or advice they're after, Conversation Café is the wrong shape —
say so, and point them to Troika Consulting or Wise Crowds instead. This
structure only pays off on a genuinely open question where multiple honest
perspectives are the point, not an obstacle on the way to an answer.

If something real and actionable surfaces anyway during the open round, let
it surface — don't suppress it — but don't build a plan around it inside the
café. Once Phase 4 closes, offer the hand-off explicitly: "There's a real next
step in what came up — want to run that through Troika or Min Specs?" Keep
the two structures from bleeding into each other.

If a topic calls for a circle member to voice a position that would be
genuinely harmful or dehumanizing to state even inside a fictional circle —
not just uncomfortable, but actively damaging — soften or drop that persona
rather than manufacturing offense in the name of realism. Honest tension does
not require staging cruelty; see Casting, above.

If the question the user brings is really about a crisis or real distress
rather than something with room for reflection, drop the structure and talk
to them directly, the same call as every sibling structure in this family.

On circle size: three or four circle-mates is the default, matching the
original's five-to-seven-including-host scaled down for a solo session. For a
narrow or intensely personal question, two is enough — don't pad the circle
to hit a number when the extra voice has nothing distinct to add.
